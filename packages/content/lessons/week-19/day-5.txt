@section math | 2 · Math: Q, decay and the exponential tail | 50 min

### Q in three ways
For a lightly damped oscillator (ζ small), one number Q = 1 ÷ 2ζ describes three things:
1. **Resonance height:** at ω₀ the amplitude is Q times the static displacement.
2. **Resonance width:** the half-power bandwidth is **Δω ≈ ω₀ ÷ Q**: a high Q is a sharp, narrow peak.
3. **Decay:** each cycle loses a fraction **2π ÷ Q** of the energy, and the amplitude falls by e^(−π ÷ Q) per cycle.

BN: হালকা অবমন্দনে Q = 1/(2ζ) তিনটা জিনিস বলে: ১) অনুনাদে বিস্তার = Q × স্থির সরণ; ২) অর্ধ-ক্ষমতা প্রস্থ Δω ≈ ω₀/Q (বড় Q = সরু শিখর); ৩) প্রতি চক্রে শক্তি 2π/Q ভগ্নাংশ কমে, বিস্তার e^(−π/Q) গুণ।

EX: Q in real things
1. **Tuning fork:** f = 440 Hz and Q = 1000. The amplitude falls to 1/e after Q ÷ π = **318 cycles = 0.72 s**. The energy lost per cycle is 2π ÷ 1000 = **0.63%**.
2. **Bandwidth:** the same fork responds only within Δf = 440 ÷ 1000 = **0.44 Hz**: it rings only for a note almost exactly its own.
3. **A radio tuner:** at 1 MHz and Q = 100: Δf = **10 kHz**, which is the width of one AM station. A higher Q would cut the sound quality, a lower Q would let neighbouring stations in.
4. **A car suspension** with ζ = 0.612 has Q = 0.82: no real resonance peak, which is the goal.
5. **A swing** with Q = 50: each cycle loses 2π ÷ 50 = 12.6% of its energy, and the amplitude falls by e^(−π ÷ 50) = 94% per cycle (0.53 after 10 cycles). To keep it going, you add energy once per cycle in time with the swing: resonance.
6. **Shattering a glass:** a wine glass has Q of order 1000, so a sound at its natural frequency (about 500 to 1000 Hz) builds the amplitude to 1000 times the static one: it breaks. A sound at other frequencies does nothing.
/EX

### The charging curve as a differential equation
A lithium-ion phone charger works in two phases:
- **Constant current (CC):** the current is fixed (here I₀ = 1.5 A) while the battery voltage rises toward 4.2 V.
- **Constant voltage (CV):** the voltage is held at 4.2 V and the current falls: **I′ = −I ÷ τ**, so **I (t) = I₀ e^(−t ÷ τ)** until it reaches a cut-off I_c, when the charger stops. The charge delivered in the CV phase is ∫ I dt = **I₀ τ (1 − e^(−t ÷ τ))**.

BN: লিথিয়াম-আয়ন চার্জার দুই ধাপে: ধ্রুব কারেন্ট (CC) যতক্ষণ ভোল্টেজ 4.2 V এ ওঠে; তারপর ধ্রুব ভোল্টেজ (CV), কারেন্ট কমে I′ = −I/τ, I = I₀e^(−t/τ), cut-off এ পৌঁছালে বন্ধ। CV ধাপে চার্জ = I₀τ(1 − e^(−t/τ))।

EX: One full charge
A 3000 mAh phone, I₀ = 1.5 A, τ = 20 min and cut-off I_c = 0.15 A.
1. **CC phase:** it reaches about 2400 mAh (80%) at 1.5 A in 2.4 ÷ 1.5 = 1.6 h = **96 min**.
2. **CV phase time:** t = τ ln (I₀ ÷ I_c) = 20 × ln 10 = **46 min**.
3. **CV charge:** (I₀ − I_c) τ = 1.35 A × (20 ÷ 60) h = **0.45 Ah = 450 mAh**.
4. **Total:** 2400 + 450 = 2850 mAh = **95%**, in about 142 min. The last 15% takes about 1/3 of the time: this is why a phone gets to 80% quickly.
5. **Energy stored:** 3 Ah × 3.85 V = **11.55 Wh = 41.6 kJ**. With a charger efficiency of 85%, the wall supplies 11.55 ÷ 0.85 = **13.6 Wh**.
6. **A year of charging:** 365 × 13.6 Wh = **4.96 kWh**: a phone costs very little to run.
7. **After one time constant** in the CV phase (t = τ = 20 min) the current is 1.5 × e⁻¹ = **0.55 A**.

TRAP: In Q = 1 ÷ 2ζ and Δω = ω₀ ÷ Q, the formulas are for **light damping** (ζ small). In CV charging the current falls to zero only in the limit: you stop at a **cut-off**, so the CV time is τ ln (I₀ ÷ I_c) and not infinite. The charge in mAh needs minutes converted to hours.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: wiring, heating and fuses | 35 min

### Joule heating in a wire
A wire of length L, cross-section A and resistivity ρ has R = ρ L ÷ A. A current I makes **P = I² R** of heat. For a house circuit the supply is 230 V and a breaker or fuse protects the wire from overheating: **I_max = rated current**. A 16 A circuit can carry P = 230 × 16 = **3680 W**.

BN: তার: R = ρL/A, তাপ P = I²R। ঘরের সরবরাহ 230 V; ফিউজ/ব্রেকার তারকে অতিতাপ থেকে বাঁচায়। 16 A সার্কিট 230 × 16 = 3680 W টানতে পারে।

EX: A kitchen circuit
1. **Heater and kettle:** a 2000 W heater draws 2000 ÷ 230 = **8.7 A** and a 2200 W kettle draws **9.57 A**. Together they draw **18.3 A**, above a 16 A breaker: it trips (and should).
2. **The wire:** 20 m of 2.5 mm² copper (each way) has a loop length of 40 m: R = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 40 ÷ 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ = **0.272 Ω**.
3. At 16 A: the voltage drop is **4.35 V** (1.9%) and the heat in the wire is 16² × 0.272 = **69.6 W**: about 1.7 W per metre of wire, which is warm, but fine.
4. **A 1.5 mm² wire** on the same run (R = 0.453 Ω) would lose **116 W** and drop 7.25 V: too much. That is why a thin wire is limited to a lower current.
5. **A fuse is a deliberate weak point:** a thin wire that melts first. A 3 kW appliance draws 13 A, so a 13 A plug fuse is a sensible choice and a 3 A fuse would blow at once.
6. **How fast does a wire heat?** The copper in the 40 m run has a mass of 8960 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 40 = **0.896 kg**. At 69.6 W with no cooling the temperature rises by 69.6 ÷ (0.896 × 385) = **0.2 K/s**: in 100 s the run would be 20 K hotter. Cooling by the air limits it; a short circuit (hundreds of amps) would melt the wire in seconds, so the breaker must trip within a fraction of a second.

TRAP: A wire loop has **two** conductors: the length in R = ρ L ÷ A is twice the distance to the appliance. Heating goes as **I²**, not I: doubling the current quadruples the heat. And fuses protect the **wire**, not you: a person is protected by a residual-current device (RCD) that trips at about 30 mA.
