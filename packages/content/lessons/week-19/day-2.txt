# Complex roots: oscillation. Springs and pendulums. Then: resistors in series and parallel
lead: When the characteristic equation has complex roots, the solution oscillates: this is the mathematics of springs, pendulums, vibrating strings, alternating current and radio. Euler's formula from Week 12 turns the complex exponentials into sines and cosines. In physics, you now combine resistors: in a chain they add, side by side they share the current, and a clever pair in series makes the voltage divider that every sensor circuit in your electronics projects uses.

BN: বৈশিষ্ট্য সমীকরণের মূল জটিল হলে সমাধান দোলে: এটাই স্প্রিং, দোলক, কম্পমান তার, পরিবর্তী প্রবাহ ও রেডিওর গণিত। সপ্তাহ ১২ এর অয়লারের সূত্র জটিল সূচককে সাইন ও কোসাইনে বদলে দেয়। পদার্থবিজ্ঞানে এবার রোধ জুড়বে: সারিতে যোগ হয়, পাশাপাশি প্রবাহ ভাগ হয়, আর সারিতে দুটো রোধ ভোল্টেজ ডিভাইডার বানায় যা তোমার ইলেকট্রনিক্স প্রজেক্টের প্রতিটি সেন্সর সার্কিটে থাকে।

@section math | 2 · Math: oscillation from complex roots | 55 min

### Complex roots
If a r² + b r + c = 0 has the complex roots r = α ± i β (when b² < 4ac), then e^((α ± iβ)x) = e^(αx) (cos βx ± i sin βx), and the real solutions are

EQ: y = e^(αx) [C₁ cos βx + C₂ sin βx]

α sets the **growth or decay** of the amplitude (α < 0 is a decay) and β the **angular frequency**. With α = 0 the oscillation never changes: **simple harmonic motion**.

BN: মূল r = α ± iβ হলে y = e^(αx)[C₁ cos βx + C₂ sin βx]। α < 0 হলে বিস্তার কমে (অবমন্দন), β = কৌণিক কম্পাঙ্ক। α = 0 হলে সরল ছন্দিত গতি (SHM), যার বিস্তার বদলায় না।

EX: Oscillations
1. **y″ + 4y = 0, y (0) = 1, y′ (0) = 0.** r = ±2i: y = C₁ cos 2x + C₂ sin 2x. C₁ = 1 and y′ (0) = 2 C₂ = 0: **y = cos 2x**, period π.
2. **A mass on a spring:** m = 0.5 kg, k = 200 N/m: m x″ + k x = 0 gives ω = √(k ÷ m) = √400 = **20 rad/s**, T = 2π ÷ 20 = **0.314 s**, f = 3.18 Hz. Pulled by 0.1 m and released: x = 0.1 cos 20t, the maximum speed is A ω = **2 m/s**, and the energy is ½ k A² = **1 J**.
3. **y″ + 2y′ + 5y = 0, y (0) = 1, y′ (0) = 0.** r = (−2 ± √(4 − 20)) ÷ 2 = −1 ± 2i: y = e^(−x) (C₁ cos 2x + C₂ sin 2x). C₁ = 1, and y′ (0) = −C₁ + 2 C₂ = 0 gives C₂ = 1/2: **y = e^(−x) (cos 2x + ½ sin 2x)**. In amplitude form this is 1.118 e^(−x) cos (2x − 26.6°). The period is π, and y (1) = e⁻¹ (cos 2 + 0.5 sin 2) = **0.0142**. The amplitude falls by e each unit of x.
4. **Amplitude and phase:** C₁ cos βx + C₂ sin βx = A cos (βx − φ), with A = √(C₁² + C₂²) and tan φ = C₂ ÷ C₁.
5. **A small-angle pendulum:** θ″ = −(g ÷ L) θ, so ω = √(g ÷ L) and **T = 2π √(L ÷ g)**, independent of the mass and (for small swings) of the amplitude. For L = 1 m: T = **2.007 s**. For L = 0.25 m: **1.004 s**. The "seconds pendulum" of a grandfather clock (half-period 1 s) has L = 0.994 m. On the Moon (g = 1.62 m/s²) the 1 m pendulum has T = **4.94 s**.
6. **Large swings** are slower: T ≈ T₀ (1 + θ₀² ÷ 16), so a swing of 0.5 rad (28.6°) is 1.6% slower, and 1 rad (57°) about 6.6% slower (exact: 1.0663).

TRAP: In the solution e^(αx) [C₁ cos βx + C₂ sin βx], β is the **imaginary part** of the root, and the frequency of the damped oscillation is **β**, not the natural frequency √(c ÷ a): they differ when there is damping. The pendulum formula is for **small** angles and measured in **radians** (not degrees). And the period of a spring or pendulum does not depend on the amplitude (isochronism), only because of linearity: for large swings it does.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: resistors in series and parallel | 35 min

### Series and parallel
**Series** (one after another): the **same current** flows through all, and the voltages add. **Parallel** (side by side): the **same voltage** across all, and the currents add.

EQ: R_series = R₁ + R₂ + …     1 ÷ R_parallel = 1 ÷ R₁ + 1 ÷ R₂ + …

Parallel is always **smaller than the smallest** resistor. (Capacitors in Week 18 follow the opposite rule.)

BN: শ্রেণি: একই প্রবাহ, ভোল্টেজ যোগ; R = R₁ + R₂ + …। সমান্তরাল: একই ভোল্টেজ, প্রবাহ যোগ; 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …, ফল সবচেয়ে ছোটটির চেয়েও ছোট। (ধারকের নিয়মের উল্টো।)

EX: Combinations
1. **6 Ω and 3 Ω:** series **9 Ω**; parallel 1 ÷ R = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2, so **2 Ω**. Two equal 10 Ω resistors in parallel give **5 Ω**.
2. **A network:** R₁ = 4 Ω in series with (6 Ω ∥ 12 Ω). The parallel pair is 6 × 12 ÷ 18 = 4 Ω. The total is **8 Ω**. With 24 V: I = **3 A**. The voltage across the pair is 3 × 4 = **12 V**, so the currents are 12 ÷ 6 = **2 A** and 12 ÷ 12 = **1 A** (2 + 1 = 3 ✓).
3. **Three in parallel (2, 3, 6 Ω):** 1 ÷ R = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1, so **R = 1 Ω**.
4. **Voltage divider:** two resistors in series across V_in; the output across R₂ is V_out = V_in R₂ ÷ (R₁ + R₂). With V_in = 12 V, R₁ = 14 kΩ, R₂ = 10 kΩ: V_out = 12 × 10 ÷ 24 = **5.0 V**. (A sensor like an LDR or thermistor replaces one resistor, and the microcontroller reads V_out.)
5. **Loading effect:** connect a 10 kΩ load across R₂. The parallel pair is 5 kΩ, so V_out = 12 × 5 ÷ (14 + 5) = **3.16 V** instead of 5 V: a divider is accurate only if the load is much larger than R₂.
6. **LED with a series resistor:** a 5 V supply, an LED that needs 2.0 V and 20 mA: R = (5 − 2) ÷ 0.02 = **150 Ω** and the resistor dissipates 0.02² × 150 = **0.06 W**.
7. **Wheatstone bridge:** balanced (no current in the detector) when R₁ ÷ R₂ = R₃ ÷ R_x. For R₁ = 100 Ω, R₂ = 200 Ω and R₃ = 150 Ω: R_x = R₃ R₂ ÷ R₁ = **300 Ω** (the way strain gauges are read).
8. **Batteries:** four 1.5 V cells in series give **6 V**; in parallel they give 1.5 V with four times the capacity (at the same current each cell supplies a quarter).
9. **Home wiring** uses **parallel** circuits: each appliance gets 230 V, and switching one off does not affect the others. The old Christmas-light strings were in series: one dead bulb broke the circuit.

TRAP: Resistors in **parallel**: 1 ÷ R_total = Σ 1 ÷ R, not Σ R. Do not forget to take the **reciprocal** at the end. In a series circuit the **voltage** splits in proportion to R, in a parallel one the **current** splits in inverse proportion to R. A voltage divider loaded by a small resistance does **not** give the unloaded V_out.

### Recap
- Complex roots α ± iβ: y = e^(αx) (C₁ cos βx + C₂ sin βx) = amplitude A e^(αx) cos (βx − φ). Spring T = 2π √(m ÷ k), pendulum T = 2π √(L ÷ g) (small angles, radians; larger swing slower by θ₀² ÷ 16). SHM energy ½ k A².
