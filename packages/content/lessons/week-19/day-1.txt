# Second-order differential equations: real roots. Then: current, resistance and Ohm's law
lead: Newton's second law says force equals mass times acceleration, and acceleration is a second derivative. So the equations of motion of springs, pendulums, circuits and bridges are second-order differential equations. For the linear ones with constant coefficients there is a beautiful trick: guess y = e^(rx) and the differential equation turns into a quadratic. In physics, you now start electric circuits: current, resistance, Ohm's law and electric power, the basic vocabulary of every device you use.

BN: নিউটনের দ্বিতীয় সূত্র বলে বল = ভর × ত্বরণ, আর ত্বরণ দ্বিতীয় অবকল। তাই স্প্রিং, দোলক, বর্তনী ও সেতুর গতির সমীকরণ দ্বিতীয় ক্রমের অবকল সমীকরণ। ধ্রুব সহগের রৈখিক সমীকরণের জন্য একটা সুন্দর কৌশল আছে: y = e^(rx) ধরে নাও, তাহলে অবকল সমীকরণ একটা দ্বিঘাত সমীকরণ হয়ে যায়। পদার্থবিজ্ঞানে এবার বৈদ্যুতিক বর্তনী শুরু: তড়িৎ প্রবাহ, রোধ, ওহমের সূত্র ও বৈদ্যুতিক ক্ষমতা, তোমার ব্যবহৃত প্রতিটি ডিভাইসের মূল শব্দভাণ্ডার।

@section math | 2 · Math: second-order linear equations | 55 min

### The form and the trick
A **second-order linear equation with constant coefficients** looks like

EQ: a y″ + b y′ + c y = 0

Try y = e^(rx). Then y′ = r e^(rx) and y″ = r² e^(rx), so a r² + b r + c = 0 after dividing out e^(rx) (which is never zero). This is the **characteristic equation**. Its roots give the solutions.

**Two real distinct roots r₁ ≠ r₂:** both e^(r₁ x) and e^(r₂ x) are solutions, and so is any combination (**superposition**, because the equation is linear):

EQ: y = C₁ e^(r₁ x) + C₂ e^(r₂ x)

There are two constants because the equation is second order: you need **two initial conditions**, y (0) and y′ (0).

BN: ay″ + by′ + cy = 0 এ y = e^(rx) ধরলে ar² + br + c = 0 (বৈশিষ্ট্য সমীকরণ)। দুটি ভিন্ন বাস্তব মূল r₁, r₂ হলে সাধারণ সমাধান y = C₁e^(r₁x) + C₂e^(r₂x)। দ্বিতীয় ক্রম বলে দুটো ধ্রুবক, তাই দুটো শুরুর শর্ত লাগে: y(0) ও y′(0)।

EX: Real roots
1. **y″ − 5y′ + 6y = 0.** r² − 5r + 6 = 0 gives r = 2 and 3: **y = C₁ e^(2x) + C₂ e^(3x)**.
2. **y″ + 3y′ + 2y = 0, y (0) = 1, y′ (0) = 0.** r² + 3r + 2 = 0 gives r = −1 and −2: y = C₁ e^(−x) + C₂ e^(−2x). Conditions: C₁ + C₂ = 1 and −C₁ − 2C₂ = 0. So C₂ = −1 and C₁ = 2: **y = 2 e^(−x) − e^(−2x)**. y (1) = 2 e⁻¹ − e⁻² = 0.7358 − 0.1353 = **0.6004**. The solution decays without oscillating.
3. **y″ − 4y = 0, y (0) = 0, y′ (0) = 2.** r = ±2: y = C₁ e^(2x) + C₂ e^(−2x). C₁ + C₂ = 0 and 2C₁ − 2C₂ = 2 give C₁ = 1/2, C₂ = −1/2: **y = sinh 2x**. y (1) = **3.627**. Growth: this is the case of an unstable system (the opposite of a spring).
4. **y″ + 5y′ + 4y = 0, y (0) = 2, y′ (0) = −5.** r² + 5r + 4 = 0 gives r = −1, −4: y = C₁ e^(−x) + C₂ e^(−4x). C₁ + C₂ = 2 and −C₁ − 4C₂ = −5 give C₂ = 1, C₁ = 1: **y = e^(−x) + e^(−4x)**, with y (0.5) = 0.6065 + 0.1353 = **0.742**. This is a heavily damped spring (m = 1, damping 5, spring 4).
5. **Check linear independence:** for e^(−x) and e^(−2x) the Wronskian W = y₁ y₂′ − y₁′ y₂ = −2 e^(−3x) + e^(−3x) = −e^(−3x) ≠ 0: they are independent, so together they give **all** solutions.

### Where the equation comes from
EX: Newton's law gives a second-order equation
1. **A mass on a spring with damping:** m x″ = −k x − b x′, i.e. m x″ + b x′ + k x = 0. With m = 1 kg, k = 4 N/m, b = 5 kg/s it is the equation of example 4 above: the mass returns to rest slowly without overshooting (**overdamped**).
2. **A car door closer, a hydraulic damper:** designed for the same overdamped behaviour, so that the door does not bang.
3. **Unstable systems:** x″ = 4x (an inverted pendulum, a pencil balanced on its tip) has the growing solution e^(2t): small errors double every ln 2 ÷ 2 = 0.35 s: why balancing a broom takes constant corrections.
4. **Charge flows:** the same equation describes charge in an RLC circuit (Week 20), with inductance in the place of mass.
/EX

TRAP: Do not forget that the roots r can be negative: e^(−x) decays. The two constants C₁ and C₂ are found from **both** conditions y (0) and y′ (0): using only one leaves a family of solutions. And only **linear** equations obey superposition: for y″ = y² you cannot add two solutions.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: current, resistance and Ohm's law | 35 min

### Current, resistance, power
**Current** is the rate at which charge crosses a section of a wire: **I = dQ/dt** (unit: ampere, 1 A = 1 C/s). In a conductor with n free charges per cubic metre, each of charge q, moving with the **drift velocity** v_d through a cross-section A: **I = n q v_d A**.

**Ohm's law** for many materials: **V = I R**. The **resistance** of a wire of length L and cross-section A is **R = ρ L ÷ A**, with ρ the resistivity (unit Ω m). **Power**: P = I V = I² R = V² ÷ R. Energy in time t: E = P t.

BN: তড়িৎ প্রবাহ I = dQ/dt (অ্যাম্পিয়ার)। I = nqv_dA, v_d = প্রবাহ বেগ। ওহমের সূত্র V = IR। তারের রোধ R = ρL/A। ক্ষমতা P = IV = I²R = V²/R; শক্তি E = Pt।

EX: First numbers
1. **Charge and electrons:** 2 A for 10 s carries Q = 20 C, which is 20 ÷ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.25 × 10²⁰ electrons**. A current of 1 A is **6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons per second**.
2. **Drift velocity.** Copper has n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ electrons/m³. In a wire of cross-section 1.5 mm² with I = 10 A: v_d = I ÷ (n q A) = 10 ÷ (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.5 × 10⁻⁶) = **4.9 × 10⁻⁴ m/s = 0.49 mm/s**. The electrons crawl; yet the lamp lights instantly, because the **electric field** is set up through the wire at close to the speed of light and pushes **all** electrons at once.
3. **A copper wire** (ρ = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m), 10 m long, 1.5 mm²: R = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 10 ÷ 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ = **0.113 Ω**. At 10 A the voltage drop is 1.13 V and the heat is I² R = **11.3 W**.
4. **A bulb:** 60 W at 230 V draws I = 60 ÷ 230 = **0.261 A** and has R = V² ÷ P = **882 Ω** (hot). A 2 kW kettle draws **8.70 A** (R = 26.4 Ω).
5. **A kettle:** to heat 1 L of water (1 kg) from 20 °C to 100 °C needs 1 × 4186 × 80 = 334.9 kJ. At 2 kW with no loss: **167 s = 2.8 minutes**. (Week 13 meets Week 19.)
6. **Resistance and temperature:** metals increase with T: R = R₀ (1 + α ΔT), with α = 0.0039 per °C for copper. A copper coil of 10.0 Ω at 20 °C that reads 11.17 Ω has ΔT = (1.117 − 1) ÷ 0.0039 = 30 °C, so is at **50 °C** (a way to measure a motor's temperature).
7. **A battery with internal resistance:** a 12 V car battery with r = 0.02 Ω delivering 200 A to the starter has a terminal voltage of V = ε − I r = 12 − 4 = **8 V**, and the internal resistance wastes I² r = **800 W**.
8. **An undersized extension cord:** 20 m of 1.0 mm² copper (40 m for the round trip) carrying 10 A: R = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 40 ÷ 10⁻⁶ = **0.68 Ω**, the drop is **6.8 V** and the cord dissipates **68 W** along its length, enough to get warm: that is why cords have a stated maximum length and current.

TRAP: Ohm's law V = I R is a property of **some materials** (ohmic), not a law of nature: diodes, bulbs and transistors are non-ohmic (R changes with V or T). P = I² R and P = V²/R are the same thing, but the second is for a fixed supply voltage, the first for a fixed current. The **conventional current** direction is that of **positive** charge flow (opposite to the electrons). And the electrons do not carry the energy: the **field** does.
