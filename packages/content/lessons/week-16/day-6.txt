LVL: Part A | Questions 1 to 11
P: (1 mark) Find f_x and f_y at (1, 2) for f = x² y³. => f_x = 2x y³ = **16**; f_y = 3x² y² = **12**.
P: (1 mark) For f = x³ y² find f_xy and check that it equals f_yx. => f_x = 3x² y², f_xy = **6x² y**. Also f_y = 2x³ y, f_yx = **6x² y** ✓.
P: (2 marks) Find the tangent plane of f = x² + y² at (1, 2) and use it to estimate f (1.1, 1.9). => f = 5, f_x = 2, f_y = 4: **L = 5 + 2 (x − 1) + 4 (y − 2)** (1 mark). L (1.1, 1.9) = 5 + 0.2 − 0.4 = **4.8** (1 mark). (Exact: 1.21 + 3.61 = 4.82.)
P: (1 mark) Find dz/dt for z = x y² with x = t² and y = t, and check by substituting. => y² · 2t + 2x y · 1 = t² · 2t + 2 t² · t = **4t³**. Direct: z = t² t² = t⁴, dz/dt = 4t³ ✓.
P: (1 mark) Find dy/dx on the circle x² + y² = 25 at (3, 4). => −F_x ÷ F_y = −6 ÷ 8 = **−3/4**.
P: (2 marks) For f = x² y at (2, 1) find ∇f, the directional derivative towards (3, 4) ÷ 5, and the largest possible rate of change. => ∇f = (2xy, x²) = **(4, 4)** (1 mark). D_u f = (4 × 3 + 4 × 4) ÷ 5 = 28 ÷ 5 = **5.6**; maximum rate |∇f| = 4√2 = **5.66** (1 mark).
P: (1 mark) The hill h = 50 − x² − y² (metres) has a gradient at (3, 4). Find the steepest slope and the direction of steepest descent. => ∇h = (−6, −8), slope **10 m per unit**; descent along (6, 8) ÷ 10 = **(0.6, 0.8)** (directly away from the summit).
P: (1 mark) Find and classify the critical point of f = x² + y² − 4x + 6y. => f_x = 2x − 4 = 0, f_y = 2y + 6 = 0: **(2, −3)**. D = 4 > 0, f_xx = 2 > 0: **minimum**, f = 4 + 9 − 8 − 18 = **−13**.
P: (2 marks) Find and classify the critical points of f = x³ − 3 x y + y³. => f_x = 3x² − 3y = 0 and f_y = 3y² − 3x = 0 give y = x² and x⁴ = x: **(0, 0) and (1, 1)** (1 mark). D = 36 x y − 9: at (0, 0) D = −9 < 0: **saddle**; at (1, 1) D = 27 > 0 and f_xx = 6 > 0: **minimum**, f = −1 (1 mark).
P: (1 mark) Use a Lagrange multiplier to maximise f = x y subject to 2x + y = 12. => (y, x) = λ (2, 1): y = 2λ, x = λ; 2λ + 2λ = 12 gives λ = 3, **x = 3, y = 6, f = 18**.
P: (1 mark) Fit y = m x + c by least squares to (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 3). => Σx = 3, Σy = 4, Σx² = 5, Σxy = 7, n = 3. 5m + 3c = 7 and 3m + 3c = 4. Subtracting: 2m = 3, **m = 1.5**, **c = −0.5**.

P: (1 mark) Find the force between +3 μC and −2 μC at 15 cm and say whether it attracts or repels. => F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 6 × 10⁻¹² ÷ 0.0225 = **2.40 N, attractive**.

P: (1 mark) Find the force and acceleration of an electron in a field of 4000 N/C. => F = e E = **6.41 × 10⁻¹⁶ N**; a = F ÷ m = **7.03 × 10¹⁴ m/s²** (opposite to the field).

P: (1 mark) A conducting sphere of radius 3 cm is charged until air breaks down at its surface (3 × 10⁶ V/m). Find its charge and potential. => Q = E R² ÷ k = 3 × 10⁶ × 9 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 8.99 × 10⁹ = **3.0 × 10⁻⁷ C = 300 nC**. V = E R = **90 kV**.

### Challenges of the week
LVL: Challenge | The field is the gradient of a potential
P: For a point charge Q define V (x, y, z) = k Q ÷ r with r = √(x² + y² + z²). Show that E = −∇V, and check for Q = 1 nC at (0.1, 0, 0). => ∂V/∂x = −k Q x ÷ r³ (and similarly for y, z), so ∇V = −k Q (x, y, z) ÷ r³ = **−E** (the field is k Q ÷ r² along the unit vector (x, y, z) ÷ r). At (0.1, 0, 0): V = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.1 = **89.9 V**, ∂V/∂x = −k Q ÷ r² = −899, so **E_x = +899 V/m** ✓. This is the key idea of Week 17: V is a scalar function that holds all the information about E.

LVL: Challenge | Making three charges balance
P: Two charges +q at x = 0 and +4q at x = 1 m are held fixed. Find the point where a third charge feels zero force, and the charge to put there so that all three are in equilibrium. => Zero field where 1 ÷ x² = 4 ÷ (1 − x)²: 1 − x = 2x, **x = 1/3 m**. For the charges to be in equilibrium the middle charge Q′ must attract both outward-pushed charges: the force on q from 4q is k · 4q² ÷ 1 (pushing it towards −x), balanced by Q′ at distance 1/3: k q |Q′| ÷ (1/9) = 9 k q |Q′|. So 9 |Q′| = 4q and **Q′ = −4q ÷ 9**. (Check the 4q charge: from q, k · 4q² ÷ 1 = 4kq²; from Q′ at 2/3: k · 4q · (4q/9) ÷ (4/9) = 4kq² ✓.) The equilibrium is unstable (the Earnshaw theorem says no purely electric equilibrium of point charges is stable).

### What Week 17 looks like
**Multivariable calculus 2 and Gauss's law.** In maths, you add up over areas and volumes with double and triple integrals, then meet line integrals, divergence and curl: the language of fields. In physics, Gauss's law gives electric fields from symmetry in a few lines, and electric potential turns a vector problem into a single number at each point. The story: why lightning rods work.
