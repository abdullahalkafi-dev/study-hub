# Lagrange multipliers: optimising under a constraint. Then: conductors, shielding and sharp points
lead: Real optimisation always has a catch: the box must have a fixed surface area, the budget is limited, the point must stay on a curve. Lagrange found a beautiful way to handle it: at the best point the gradient of what you optimise lines up with the gradient of the constraint. In physics, you meet conductors: the metal in which charges move freely. That one fact explains why a car is safe in a thunderstorm, why electronics are shielded in metal boxes, and why lightning rods have sharp tips.

BN: বাস্তব অপটিমাইজেশনে সবসময় একটা শর্ত থাকে: বাক্সের পৃষ্ঠতল নির্দিষ্ট, বাজেট সীমিত, বিন্দুকে বক্ররেখার উপরে থাকতে হবে। ল্যাগ্রাঞ্জ চমৎকার উপায় বের করেছেন: সেরা বিন্দুতে যে রাশিটা সর্বোচ্চ বা সর্বনিম্ন করছ তার গ্রেডিয়েন্ট শর্তের গ্রেডিয়েন্টের সাথে এক সারিতে পড়ে। পদার্থবিজ্ঞানে পরিবাহী (যে ধাতুতে চার্জ মুক্তভাবে চলে) পড়বে। এই একটা তথ্যই বোঝায় কেন ঝড়ে গাড়ি নিরাপদ, কেন ইলেকট্রনিক্স ধাতুর বাক্সে ঢাকা থাকে, আর কেন বজ্ররোধক দণ্ডের মাথা সূচালো।

@section math | 2 · Math: Lagrange multipliers | 55 min

### The idea
Maximise or minimise f (x, y) while staying on the curve g (x, y) = c. Picture the level curves of f and the constraint curve. As you walk along the constraint, f rises and falls. At the best point the constraint curve just **touches** a level curve of f, without crossing it, so the two curves have the same tangent. Their normals, the gradients, are **parallel**:

EQ: ∇f = λ ∇g     together with     g (x, y) = c

These are three equations for the three unknowns x, y, λ. The number λ is the **Lagrange multiplier**.

BN: শর্ত g(x, y) = c এর উপর f সর্বোচ্চ/সর্বনিম্ন করতে: সেরা বিন্দুতে শর্তের বক্ররেখা f এর একটা সমোন্নতি রেখাকে ঠিক স্পর্শ করে, তাই দুটোর গ্রেডিয়েন্ট সমান্তরাল: ∇f = λ∇g এবং g = c। অজানা তিনটি: x, y, λ।

EX: Four constrained problems
1. **Largest product with a fixed sum.** Maximise f = x y with x + y = 10. ∇f = (y, x) and ∇g = (1, 1): y = λ and x = λ, so x = y, and then x = y = **5**, f = **25**, λ = 5.
2. **Closest point on a line.** Minimise f = x² + y² (squared distance) subject to x + 2y = 5. (2x, 2y) = λ (1, 2): x = λ ÷ 2 and y = λ. Then λ ÷ 2 + 2λ = 5, λ = 2, so **(x, y) = (1, 2)**, f = **5**, and the distance is √5 = **2.236**. (Check: |5| ÷ √(1 + 4) = √5 ✓.)
3. **The biggest rectangle in an ellipse.** Maximise the area 4 x y of a rectangle with its corners on x² ÷ 9 + y² ÷ 4 = 1. (y, x) = λ (2x ÷ 9, 2y ÷ 4). From the first, y = 2λ x ÷ 9; from the second x = λ y ÷ 2. Combine: y = (2λ ÷ 9)(λ y ÷ 2) = λ² y ÷ 9, so λ² = 9, **λ = 3**. Then x = 1.5 y, and 2.25 y² ÷ 9 + y² ÷ 4 = 0.5 y² = 1: **y = √2 = 1.414, x = 2.121**. The maximum area is 4 × 2.121 × 1.414 = **12** (the general result is 2 a b = 12 ✓).
4. **The best box.** Maximise V = x y z for a closed box with surface area 2 (x y + y z + z x) = 600 cm². The equations (y z, x z, x y) = 2λ (y + z, x + z, x + y) force x = y = z, so **x = y = z = 10 cm** and V = **1000 cm³**. The cube is best.
/EX

### What λ means
The multiplier is the **price of the constraint**: if you relax the constraint from c to c + dc, the optimum changes by about **λ dc**.

EX: λ as a shadow price
1. **Example 1 above** has f* = (c ÷ 2)² = c² ÷ 4 (for sum c). So df*/dc = c ÷ 2 = **5 = λ** at c = 10 ✓.
2. **Budget:** a shopper maximises U = √(x y) with prices 2 and 4 and a budget of 40: 2x + 4y = 40. ∇U = (0.5 √(y ÷ x), 0.5 √(x ÷ y)) = λ (2, 4). Dividing: y ÷ x = 2 ÷ 4, so **y = x ÷ 2** and 2x + 2x = 40, **x = 10, y = 5**, U = √50 = **7.07**. The multiplier is λ = 0.5 √0.5 ÷ 2 = **0.177**: one extra unit of budget raises the satisfaction by 0.177 units.
3. **Engineering:** λ tells you how much it is worth to relax a constraint (a larger area, a heavier limit).
/EX

### Eigenvalues are a Lagrange problem
Maximise f = xᵀ A x (a quadratic form) on the unit circle g = xᵀ x = 1. ∇f = 2 A x and ∇g = 2 x, so the condition ∇f = λ ∇g reads **A x = λ x**: the multipliers are the **eigenvalues** and the solutions the eigenvectors. The maximum of the form is the largest eigenvalue. This closes the loop with last week: for A = [[5, 2], [2, 2]] the maximum on the circle is **6** and the minimum **1**.

BN: f = xᵀAx কে একক বৃত্তে সর্বোচ্চ করতে ∇f = λ∇g থেকে Ax = λx পাওয়া যায়: গুণক = eigenvalue। সর্বোচ্চ মান = বৃহত্তম eigenvalue। A = [[5, 2], [2, 2]] এর জন্য সর্বোচ্চ 6, সর্বনিম্ন 1।

TRAP: Do not forget the **constraint equation** g = c: the equation ∇f = λ∇g alone has too many solutions. Also check which solution is the **maximum** and which the **minimum** by comparing values of f (there may be several candidate points). And if ∇g = 0 at some point on the constraint, the method can miss it.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: conductors in electrostatics | 35 min

### Free charges rearrange until E = 0 inside
In a conductor the charges move freely. If there were a field inside, they would move; they stop only when the field is gone. In electrostatic equilibrium:

1. **E = 0 inside** the conductor.
2. Any **excess charge** sits on the **surface**.
3. The field at the surface is **perpendicular** to the surface and has the size **E = σ ÷ ε₀**.
4. The whole conductor is at the **same potential**.

BN: পরিবাহীতে চার্জ মুক্ত। ক্ষেত্র থাকলে চার্জ সরবে, তাই সাম্যে: ভিতরে E = 0; অতিরিক্ত চার্জ পৃষ্ঠে; পৃষ্ঠে ক্ষেত্র লম্ব এবং E = σ/ε₀; পুরো পরিবাহী একই বিভবে।

EX: Shielding
1. **A hollow conductor** (a closed box, a car body, a mesh) has **E = 0 inside the metal**, and if the cavity has no charge, **E = 0 in the cavity**: external fields cannot reach inside (Faraday cage). In a thunderstorm the charges of the lightning strike flow around the **outside** of the car body, so the passengers are safe (do not touch the metal).
2. **A charge in a cavity:** a +5 nC charge at the centre of a spherical metal shell (inner radius 10 cm, outer radius 12 cm). The shell's inner surface gets −5 nC (σ = −5 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ (4π × 0.01) = **−3.98 × 10⁻⁸ C/m²**) and the outer surface +5 nC (σ = **+2.76 × 10⁻⁸ C/m²**). The field: at r = 5 cm (in the cavity) E = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 5 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.0025 = **17,980 N/C**; inside the metal (r = 11 cm) **E = 0**; outside at r = 20 cm: E = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 5 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.04 = **1124 N/C**, as if the shell were not there.
3. **Why electronics have metal cases:** a shield keeps out stray fields (and keeps in the interference from the circuit).
/EX

### Sharp points and the lightning rod
Two conducting spheres joined by a wire are at the **same potential**: k Q₁ ÷ R₁ = k Q₂ ÷ R₂. So Q₁ ÷ R₁ = Q₂ ÷ R₂, and the **surface charge density** σ = Q ÷ 4π R² is **inversely proportional to R**: σ₁ ÷ σ₂ = R₂ ÷ R₁. The smaller sphere (the sharper surface) carries a much larger σ and therefore a much larger field E = σ ÷ ε₀.

EX: Two joined spheres
R₁ = 10 cm and R₂ = 1 cm with a total charge of 11 nC.
1. **The charges split in proportion to the radii:** Q₁ = 10 nC and Q₂ = 1 nC. The common potential is V = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 10⁻⁸ ÷ 0.1 = **899 V**.
2. **Surface densities:** σ₁ = 10⁻⁸ ÷ (4π × 0.01) = **7.96 × 10⁻⁸ C/m²** and σ₂ = 10⁻⁹ ÷ (4π × 10⁻⁴) = **7.96 × 10⁻⁷ C/m²**, ten times larger.
3. **Fields at the surfaces:** E₁ = kQ₁ ÷ R₁² = **8990 N/C** and E₂ = kQ₂ ÷ R₂² = **89,900 N/C**, ten times larger.
4. **Sparks:** air breaks down at 3 × 10⁶ V/m, so a sphere of radius R reaches breakdown at V = E R. For R = 1 cm: **30 kV**; for R = 10 cm: **300 kV**. A sharp point sparks at far lower voltages (**corona discharge**): this is the principle of the lightning rod. The pointed rod leaks charge into the air gently and gives lightning an easy path to ground. It is also why high-voltage equipment has smooth rounded shapes: they avoid sparks.
/EX

TRAP: The field **just outside** a conductor is σ ÷ ε₀, but the field of an infinite **sheet** (not a conductor) is σ ÷ 2ε₀. The difference is that a conductor's charge sits on **one** side, and the field inside is zero. Do not say "charge lives inside a hollow conductor": it lives on the **surface** (inner surface if there is a charge in the cavity). And a Faraday cage shields electric fields, not static **magnetic** fields.
