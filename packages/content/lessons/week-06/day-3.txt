### The natural logarithm
You found it yesterday: from e^(ln x) = x and the chain rule, (ln x)′ = 1/x. With the chain rule for ln(u):

EQ: d/dx ln(u) = u′ ÷ u

For other bases, use log_a x = ln x ÷ ln a, so (log_a x)′ = 1 ÷ (x ln a). Notice how special ln is: the only base where the constant is 1.

LAB: The slope of ln x | The slope of y = ln x at x₀ should be 1/x₀. Move x₀ and compare the numerical slope with 1/x₀. The slope is steep near 0 and flattens as x grows, which is why logs grow so slowly. | x0:Point x₀:0.2:5:0.1:1 | (log(x0+0.000001)-log(x0))/0.000001:Numerical slope:3 ;; 1/x0:Predicted slope 1/x₀:3 | log(x) ;; (1/x0)*(x-x0)+log(x0) | 0,6 | -3,3

EX: Logs with the chain rule
1. d/dx ln(3x + 1) = 3 ÷ (3x + 1).
2. d/dx ln(sin x) = cos x ÷ sin x = **cot x**.
3. d/dx (x ln x) = ln x + x × (1/x) = **ln x + 1**. This is zero at x = 1/e, where x ln x has its minimum value −1/e = −0.368.
4. d/dx (ln x ÷ x) = (1 − ln x) ÷ x². Zero at x = e, the maximum of ln x ÷ x, with value 1/e.
/EX

BN: ln x এর অন্তরকলজ 1/x, আর ln(u) এর u′/u। যেকোনো ভিত্তি a এর জন্য log_a x এর অন্তরকলজ 1/(x ln a)। ln x খাড়া হয় 0 এর কাছে আর সমতল হয় x বাড়লে, তাই লগারিদম খুব ধীরে বাড়ে।

### The other trig functions
Use the quotient rule on sin and cos:
- tan x = sin x ÷ cos x gives sec²x (Monday).
- sec x = 1 ÷ cos x. Chain rule on (cos x)⁻¹: −(cos x)⁻² × (−sin x) = sin x ÷ cos²x = **sec x tan x**.
- cot x = cos x ÷ sin x gives −1 ÷ sin²x = **−csc²x**.

### The inverse trig functions: a beautiful trick
**arcsin.** Let y = arcsin x, which means sin y = x with y between −π/2 and π/2. Differentiate both sides with respect to x: cos y × y′ = 1, so y′ = 1 ÷ cos y. Since cos y ≥ 0 on this range, cos y = √(1 − sin²y) = √(1 − x²). So:

EQ: d/dx arcsin x = 1 ÷ √(1 − x²)

**arctan.** Let y = arctan x, so tan y = x. Then sec²y × y′ = 1, and sec²y = 1 + tan²y = 1 + x². So:

EQ: d/dx arctan x = 1 ÷ (1 + x²)

(The same trick gives arccos x with a minus sign: −1 ÷ √(1 − x²).)

FUNC: arctan and its slope | x | y | atan(x) ;; 1/(1+x**2) | -5,5 | -2,2 | - | Teal: y = arctan x, which levels off at ±π/2. Orange: its derivative 1 ÷ (1 + x²), a bell shape with value 1 at x = 0, where arctan is steepest, and falling toward 0 where arctan flattens.

EX: Using the inverse trig derivatives
1. d/dx arctan(2x) = 2 ÷ (1 + 4x²).
2. d/dx arcsin(x/2) = (1/2) ÷ √(1 − x²/4) = **1 ÷ √(4 − x²)**.
3. d/dx e^(sin x) = **cos x · e^(sin x)**.
4. d/dx 3ˣ = (ln 3) 3ˣ = 1.0986 × 3ˣ.
5. d/dx log₁₀ x = 1 ÷ (x ln 10) = **0.4343 ÷ x**.
/EX

### Where logs in derivatives matter
EX: pH is a logarithm
1. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Then d(pH)/d[H⁺] = −1 ÷ ([H⁺] ln 10).
2. At [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ mol/L (pure water), the slope is −1 ÷ (10⁻⁷ × 2.303) = **−4.34 × 10⁶** per mol/L. A tiny change in concentration moves the pH noticeably. At high concentration, the same change moves the pH much less, which is why logs suit quantities spanning many orders of magnitude.
/EX

TRAP: d/dx (ln x) is 1/x, not ln(1/x) or x ln x. And do not confuse (aˣ)′ = (ln a) aˣ with the power rule x^n: aˣ has a variable exponent. For arcsin, the derivative blows up at x = ±1 (a vertical tangent), where the curve is steepest.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: centre of mass and collisions in 2D | 35 min

### The centre of mass
A complicated body (a spinning wrench, a person doing a flip) has a single point whose motion is simple. This point is the **centre of mass (CM)**, the mass-weighted average position.

EQ: x_cm = (m₁ x₁ + m₂ x₂ + …) ÷ (m₁ + m₂ + …)

For a continuous body, you average over all its mass; for a uniform, symmetric body, it is at the geometric centre. (We will use integration to compute others in Week 8.)

EX: Two masses
1. 2 kg at x = 0 and 6 kg at x = 4 m. x_cm = (0 + 24) ÷ 8 = **3 m**, closer to the heavier mass.
/EX

EX: Three masses in a plane
1. 1 kg at (0, 0), 2 kg at (3, 0), 3 kg at (0, 4). x_cm = (0 + 6 + 0) ÷ 6 = 1, and y_cm = (0 + 0 + 12) ÷ 6 = 2. The CM is at **(1, 2)**.
/EX

### Motion of the centre of mass
Add Newton's second law for every particle in a system. The internal forces cancel in pairs (third law), leaving only the **external** force:

EQ: F_ext = M a_cm     (M = total mass)

So the centre of mass moves as if all the mass were concentrated there and all external forces acted on it. A firework shell explodes in the air, but its CM keeps following the same parabola. A diver twists and somersaults, but the CM follows a simple arc.

EX: Two people on ice
1. A 60 kg and a 40 kg person stand 5 m apart on frictionless ice and pull on a rope between them.
2. No external horizontal force, so the CM stays fixed: it is at 40 × 5 ÷ 100 = 2 m from the heavier person.
3. They meet at the CM: the 60 kg person moves **2 m** and the 40 kg person moves **3 m**.
/EX

### Collisions in two dimensions
Momentum is a vector, so it is conserved **in each direction separately**: Σp_x before = Σp_x after and Σp_y before = Σp_y after.

EX: Two cars at an intersection
1. A 1000 kg car going east at 20 m/s and a 1500 kg car going north at 10 m/s collide and stick.
2. x: 1000 × 20 = 2500 × v_x, so v_x = 8 m/s. y: 1500 × 10 = 2500 × v_y, so v_y = 6 m/s.
3. Speed = √(64 + 36) = **10 m/s**, at an angle of tan⁻¹(6/8) = **36.9° north of east**.
/EX

### A beautiful result: equal masses, elastic, one at rest
Ball 1 (speed u) hits an identical ball 2 at rest, off-centre, elastically. Momentum: u = v₁ + v₂ (vectors). Square it using the dot product: u² = v₁² + v₂² + 2 v₁ · v₂. But energy conservation says u² = v₁² + v₂². Therefore

EQ: v₁ · v₂ = 0

**The two balls leave at a right angle.** (This is why, in billiards, the cue ball and the object ball always separate at 90° after a glancing, equal-mass collision.)

EX: A glancing collision
1. A cue ball at u = 4 m/s hits an equal ball at rest. After the collision the cue ball moves at 30° to its original line. The other ball must go at 60° on the other side.
2. By components: v₁ = u cos 30° = **3.46 m/s** and v₂ = u sin 30° = **2 m/s**.
3. Check x-momentum: 3.464 cos 30° + 2 cos 60° = 3 + 1 = 4 ✓. y-momentum: 3.464 sin 30° − 2 sin 60° = 1.732 − 1.732 = 0 ✓.
/EX

TRAP: Do not forget that **external** forces change the CM's motion: when the firework's pieces hit the ground, the ground force acts. In 2D collisions, always write two equations (x and y), and keep signs for components. A common error is to add speeds instead of vectors.
