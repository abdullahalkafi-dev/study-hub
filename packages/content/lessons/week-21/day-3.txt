# The complex Fourier series, spectra and filtering. Then: magnetic forces on wires and the field of a current
lead: The sine and cosine form of Fourier series is easier to read once you rewrite it with complex exponentials, and that form leads straight to the idea of a spectrum: a bar chart of how much of each frequency a signal contains. Pass a wave through a filter and each bar gets multiplied by the filter's gain. In physics, the magnetic force on a single charge becomes the force on a whole wire, and Biot-Savart's law lets you find the field that a current itself makes. Together these two ideas are what make a loudspeaker, a motor and an electromagnet work.

BN: সাইন-কোসাইন রূপ জটিল সূচকে লিখলে সহজ পড়া যায়, আর তা থেকে বর্ণালী (spectrum) ধারণা আসে: সংকেতে কোন কম্পাঙ্ক কতটা, তার বার চার্ট। ফিল্টারে দিলে প্রতিটা বার ফিল্টারের গেইন দিয়ে গুণ হয়। পদার্থবিজ্ঞানে একক চার্জের চৌম্বক বল পুরো তারে বল হয়ে যায়, আর বায়ো-সাভার্ট সূত্র কারেন্ট নিজে যে ক্ষেত্র বানায় তা বের করে। লাউডস্পিকার, মোটর ও ইলেকট্রোম্যাগনেট এভাবেই কাজ করে।

@section math | 2 · Math: the complex Fourier series and spectra | 55 min

### One formula instead of two
Replace cos nx and sin nx by (e^(inx) ± e^(−inx)) ÷ 2 (and ÷ 2i). The series becomes

EQ: f (x) = Σ cₙ e^(inx) for n from −∞ to ∞, cₙ = (1 ÷ 2π) ∫ f (x) e^(−inx) dx (from −π to π)

The two forms are linked: for n > 0, **cₙ = (aₙ − i bₙ) ÷ 2**, **c₋ₙ** is its complex conjugate, and c₀ = a₀ ÷ 2. The size |cₙ| is the **spectrum**: a bar at each frequency n. The phase of cₙ tells where the wave starts.

BN: f(x) = Σ cₙ e^(inx), cₙ = (1/2π)∫ f e^(−inx) dx। সম্পর্ক: n > 0 এ cₙ = (aₙ − ibₙ)/2, c₋ₙ হলো তার অনুবন্ধী। |cₙ| হলো বর্ণালী: প্রতিটা কম্পাঙ্কে একটা বার।

EX: Spectra of three waves
1. **A pure cosine cos 3x:** c₃ = c₋₃ = ½ and everything else is 0: one pair of bars at ±3.
2. **The square wave:** bₙ = 4 ÷ (nπ) for odd n, so **cₙ = −2i ÷ (nπ)**, and |c₁| = 0.637, |c₃| = 0.212, |c₅| = 0.127. The bars fall like 1 ÷ n.
3. **A pulse train** (f = 1 for |x| < a, 0 for the rest of the period): c₀ = a ÷ π and cₙ = sin (n a) ÷ (n π).
   - For a = π ÷ 2 (**50% duty**): c₀ = 0.5, c₁ = 1 ÷ π = **0.318**, c₂ = 0, c₃ = −1 ÷ (3π) = **−0.106**.
   - For a = π ÷ 4 (**25% duty**): c₀ = 0.25, c₁ = **0.225**, c₂ = **0.159**, c₃ = **0.075**, c₄ = 0, c₅ = **−0.045**. A narrow pulse has a wide spectrum: shorter in time means wider in frequency. This is the idea of the Fourier transform in Week 22.
/EX

### Filtering a wave harmonic by harmonic
A linear filter treats each harmonic separately: harmonic n of a wave with fundamental frequency f₀ gets multiplied by the filter's gain at the frequency n f₀ and shifted in phase. For the RC low-pass filter with cut-off f_c = f₀: |H| = 1 ÷ √(1 + n²), phase = −atan n.

EX: A square wave through an RC filter
A 1 kHz square wave of amplitude 1 passes through an RC low-pass with f_c = 1 kHz.
| Harmonic | Input amplitude | Gain | Output amplitude | Phase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 kHz | 1.273 | 0.707 | **0.900** | −45° |
| 3 kHz | 0.424 | 0.316 | **0.134** | −71.6° |
| 5 kHz | 0.255 | 0.196 | **0.050** | −78.7° |
| 7 kHz | 0.182 | 0.141 | **0.026** | −81.9° |
1. **The shape:** the sharp corners come from the high harmonics, which are cut hard, so the output is a rounded wave, close to a sine delayed by 45°.
2. **Distortion:** the **total harmonic distortion** is √(sum of squares of the higher harmonics) ÷ the fundamental. For the input: √(π² ÷ 8 − 1) = **48.3%**. For the output: **16.4%**: the filter makes the wave much cleaner.
3. **This is how a "tone" or "bass" knob works**, and how a class-D audio amplifier turns its fast square pulses into smooth music.
/EX

TRAP: cₙ has a factor 1 ÷ 2π, not 1 ÷ π, and the exponent is **−inx**. The spectrum |cₙ| splits each real harmonic into two bars (at n and −n), each half the size. Filtering multiplies each harmonic by the gain at **its own** frequency n f₀, not at f₀. A filter that cuts the high harmonics also delays the wave (phase) as well as rounding it.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: wires in magnetic fields and the field of a current | 35 min

### The force on a wire
A wire of length L carrying a current I through a field B feels the sum of the forces on its moving charges:

EQ: **F** = I **L** × **B**, with magnitude F = B I L sin θ

(L points along the current.) This is the force that turns the cone of a loudspeaker and the coil of a motor.

### The field of a current
A current makes a magnetic field. For a long straight wire, at distance r:

EQ: B = μ₀ I ÷ (2π r), with the field lines in circles around the wire

(right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl along **B**). The general rule is the **Biot-Savart law**: each small piece I d**l** contributes dB = (μ₀ ÷ 4π) I d**l** × r̂ ÷ r². For a **circular loop** of radius R, all the pieces are at distance R and perpendicular to r̂, so at the centre B = (μ₀ ÷ 4π) I (2πR) ÷ R² = **μ₀ I ÷ (2R)**. Along the axis at distance z: B = μ₀ I R² ÷ (2 (R² + z²)^(3/2)). A coil of N turns has N times these values.

Two parallel wires attract each other if their currents are in the **same** direction and repel if they are opposed: wire 1 makes a field B₁ = μ₀ I₁ ÷ (2π d) at wire 2, which acts on wire 2 with the force per length **F ÷ L = μ₀ I₁ I₂ ÷ (2π d)**.

BN: তারে বল: F = I L × B, মান BIL sin θ। লম্বা সোজা তারের ক্ষেত্র: B = μ₀I/(2πr)। বায়ো-সাভার্ট: dB = (μ₀/4π) I dl × r̂/r²। বৃত্তাকার লুপের কেন্দ্রে B = μ₀I/(2R); অক্ষ বরাবর B = μ₀IR²/(2(R² + z²)^(3/2))। সমান্তরাল তার: একই দিকে আকর্ষণ, উল্টো দিকে বিকর্ষণ; F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd)।

EX: Numbers for wires and coils
1. **A wire in a field:** 2 m of wire carries 10 A across a 0.5 T field: F = 0.5 × 10 × 2 = **10 N**.
2. **A loudspeaker coil:** 50 turns of radius 1.5 cm have a wire length L = 50 × 2π × 0.015 = **4.71 m** in a radial field of 1 T. With I = 1 A the force is **4.71 N**, which moves the cone. Change the sign of the current and the cone moves the other way, so an audio signal makes the cone vibrate.
3. **A hovering wire:** a horizontal wire of mass 0.05 kg per metre in a horizontal field of 0.5 T floats when B I = λ g, so I = 0.05 × 9.81 ÷ 0.5 = **0.98 A**.
4. **Field of a long wire:** 100 A at 5 cm gives B = 2 × 10⁻⁷ × 100 ÷ 0.05 = **0.4 mT**, which is 8 times Earth's field. At 1 m it is 20 μT.
5. **A flat coil:** R = 5 cm, I = 2 A and N = 100 turns: **B (centre) = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 100 × 2 ÷ (2 × 0.05) = 2.51 mT**. On the axis, 5 cm from the centre, the field is 2.51 × 2^(−3/2) = **0.89 mT**.
6. **Two cables:** two parallel wires of 10 A each, 10 cm apart, attract with F ÷ L = 2 × 10⁻⁷ × 10 × 10 ÷ 0.1 = **2 × 10⁻⁴ N/m**. This effect defines the ampere: 1 A in each of two wires 1 m apart gives 2 × 10⁻⁷ N per metre.

TRAP: Currents in the **same** direction attract (opposite to charges, which repel when alike). In F = B I L sin θ the angle is between the wire and the field, and the force is zero if the wire is parallel to B. The field of a long wire falls as 1 ÷ r, not 1 ÷ r². And μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷, so μ₀ ÷ 2π = 2 × 10⁻⁷, a useful shortcut.
