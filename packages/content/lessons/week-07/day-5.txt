### Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem
**Rolle's theorem.** If f is continuous on [a, b], differentiable on (a, b), and f(a) = f(b), then somewhere between a and b the tangent is horizontal: f′(c) = 0. (A hill or a valley must lie between two points at the same height.)

**Mean value theorem (MVT).** If f is continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b), there is a number c in (a, b) with

EQ: f′(c) = (f(b) − f(a)) ÷ (b − a)

**Picture.** Draw the chord from (a, f(a)) to (b, f(b)). Somewhere on the curve, the tangent is parallel to the chord.

**Why it is true.** Tilt the picture so the chord becomes horizontal: define g(x) = f(x) − (the chord line). Then g(a) = g(b) = 0 and Rolle's theorem gives a point where g′ = 0, which means f′ equals the slope of the chord. ∎

BN: গড় মান উপপাদ্য: জ্যা (chord) এর ঢাল = কোনো না কোনো বিন্দুতে স্পর্শকের ঢাল। রোলের উপপাদ্য: সমান উচ্চতার দুই বিন্দুর মাঝে কোথাও স্পর্শক অনুভূমিক। যেমন, এক ঘণ্টায় ১০০ কিমি গেলে কোনো মুহূর্তে গতি ঠিক ১০০ কিমি/ঘণ্টা ছিলই।

EX: Finding c
1. f(x) = x³ on [0, 2]. The chord slope is (8 − 0) ÷ 2 = 4. Solve 3c² = 4: **c = 2/√3 = 1.155**, which lies in (0, 2).
/EX

(Speed cameras use this: two time-stamped cameras and the MVT prove a driver exceeded the limit even without a measurement at the instant.)

### What the MVT tells us
- If f′ = 0 everywhere on an interval, f is **constant** there.
- If f′ > 0 on an interval, f is **increasing** (so the first derivative test works).
- If two functions have the same derivative, they differ by a constant.

EX: Exactly one root
1. Show that x³ + x − 1 = 0 has exactly one real root. Week 5 gave existence (IVT).
2. f′ = 3x² + 1 > 0 for all x, so f is strictly increasing and can cross zero only once. If there were two roots, Rolle's theorem would give a point with f′ = 0, which is impossible. **Exactly one root** (0.6823).
/EX

EX: An inequality
1. Show |sin b − sin a| ≤ |b − a|. By the MVT, sin b − sin a = cos c × (b − a) for some c, and |cos c| ≤ 1. ∎ In words: sine is never steeper than 1.
/EX

### L'Hôpital's rule
In Week 5, forms like 0 ÷ 0 required clever tricks. The MVT gives a uniform way:

**L'Hôpital's rule.** If lim f(x) ÷ g(x) is of the form 0 ÷ 0 or ∞ ÷ ∞, and lim f′(x) ÷ g′(x) exists, then

EQ: lim f(x) ÷ g(x) = lim f′(x) ÷ g′(x)

(The idea: near the point, f and g both behave like straight lines through zero, so the ratio of the functions equals the ratio of their slopes.) You may apply it repeatedly.

EX: Basic uses
1. lim (x → 0) (eˣ − 1) ÷ x is 0 ÷ 0. Differentiate: eˣ ÷ 1 → **1**.
2. lim (x → 0) (eˣ − 1 − x) ÷ x² is 0 ÷ 0. First: (eˣ − 1) ÷ 2x is still 0 ÷ 0. Again: eˣ ÷ 2 → **1/2**.
3. lim (x → 0) (1 − cos x) ÷ x² → sin x ÷ 2x → cos x ÷ 2 → **1/2** (the answer that took trickery in Week 5).
4. lim (x → ∞) ln x ÷ x is ∞ ÷ ∞: (1/x) ÷ 1 → **0**.
/EX

### Other indeterminate forms
L'Hôpital applies directly only to 0 ÷ 0 and ∞ ÷ ∞. Other forms must be rewritten.

| Form | Method |
| 0 × ∞ | Write as a quotient: f g = f ÷ (1/g) |
| ∞ − ∞ | Combine into one fraction |
| 1^∞, 0⁰, ∞⁰ | Take ln, turn it into a product, then a quotient |

EX: Rewriting
1. lim (x → ∞) x e^(−x) = lim x ÷ eˣ (∞ ÷ ∞) → 1 ÷ eˣ → **0**. The exponential wins over any power.
2. lim (x → 0⁺) x ln x = lim ln x ÷ (1/x) (∞ ÷ ∞) → (1/x) ÷ (−1/x²) = −x → **0**.
3. lim (x → 0⁺) xˣ: ln y = x ln x → 0, so y → e⁰ = **1**.
4. lim (x → ∞) (1 + 1/x)ˣ: ln y = ln(1 + 1/x) ÷ (1/x) → 0 ÷ 0, derivative: (−1/x²) ÷ (1 + 1/x) ÷ (−1/x²) = 1 ÷ (1 + 1/x) → 1, so y → **e**.
/EX

**Growth ranking.** From L'Hôpital: as x → ∞, **eˣ grows faster than any power xⁿ, and any power grows faster than ln x**. In symbols: ln x ≪ xⁿ ≪ eˣ. This is why exponential processes (viruses, interest) outrun every polynomial.

TRAP: Check that the limit really is 0 ÷ 0 or ∞ ÷ ∞ **before** applying the rule. For lim (x → 0) (x + 1) ÷ (x + 2) the answer is 1/2 by substitution; the rule would give 1, which is wrong. Also, differentiate the top and bottom **separately** (this is not the quotient rule).

### A simple truss
A **truss** is a framework of straight members joined at pins, designed so each member carries only **tension** (pull) or **compression** (push) along its length. Bridges, roofs and cranes use trusses because they carry large loads with little material.

**Method of joints.** Treat each pin as a particle in equilibrium: the forces on it (loads, supports, member forces) sum to zero in x and y.

EX: A triangular roof truss
1. A symmetric truss spans 8 m. The apex is 3 m above the middle of the bottom chord. A load of 12 kN hangs at the apex.
2. By symmetry each support carries 6 kN upward.
3. Each rafter has length √(4² + 3²) = 5 m, at an angle α to the horizontal with sin α = 3/5, cos α = 4/5.
4. Joint at the left support: vertical balance: 6 kN (support) pushes up and the rafter pushes down with F sin α, so F × 3/5 = 6 and **F = 10 kN (compression)**.
5. Horizontal balance: the bottom chord pulls the pin inward with T = F cos α = 10 × 4/5 = **8 kN (tension)**.
6. Check at the apex: two rafters push down 2 × 10 × 3/5 = 12 kN, which equals the load ✓.

**What the lab shows:** a flat roof (small h) makes both the rafter force and the chord tension large, since T = (P ÷ 2)(s/2) ÷ h. Steeper roofs need less force but more material. That is why long, shallow bridges need deep trusses or massive beams.

### Stress and strain
How strong must a member be? **Stress** is the force per area, and **strain** is the fractional stretch:

EQ: stress σ = F ÷ A     (pascal, Pa = N/m²)     strain ε = ΔL ÷ L     (no unit)

For small loads, stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law for materials):

EQ: σ = E ε     so     ΔL = F L ÷ (A E)     (E is Young's modulus)

| Material | Young's modulus E | Typical failure stress |
| Steel | 200 GPa | 400 MPa and up |
| Aluminium | 70 GPa | 200 MPa |
| Concrete (compression) | 30 GPa | 30 MPa |
| Rubber | about 0.01 GPa | stretches enormously |

EX: A loaded steel cable
1. A steel cable (cross-section 1.0 cm² = 1 × 10⁻⁴ m², length 10 m) holds a 500 kg load. F = 4900 N.
2. Stress σ = 4900 ÷ 10⁻⁴ = **49 MPa**, far below the 400 MPa failure stress. The **safety factor** is 400 ÷ 49 = **8.2**.
3. Extension ΔL = F L ÷ (A E) = 4900 × 10 ÷ (10⁻⁴ × 2 × 10¹¹) = **2.45 mm**.
4. As a spring: k = A E ÷ L = 2 × 10⁶ N/m. Steel behaves like a very stiff spring, and its elastic energy is ½ k ΔL² = **6.0 J**.
/EX

EX: The most load a rope can lift
1. A cable of area 2 cm² is rated at a maximum stress of 100 MPa for safe use. Its maximum load is 100 × 10⁶ × 2 × 10⁻⁴ = **20,000 N**, about 2000 kg. Doubling the diameter multiplies the area (and the capacity) by **four**.
/EX

BN: পীড়ন (stress) σ = F/A, বিকৃতি (strain) ε = ΔL/L, আর ছোট ভারে σ = Eε (ইয়ং গুণাঙ্ক E)। তাই ΔL = FL/(AE)। নিরাপত্তা গুণক = ভাঙার পীড়ন ÷ প্রকৃত পীড়ন। ব্যাস দ্বিগুণ করলে ক্ষমতা চার গুণ।

TRAP: In truss problems, decide each member's direction by the joint: a member that pulls a pin away is in tension, and one that pushes into it is in compression. A common slip is to forget the support reactions, which you must find first from whole-body equilibrium. And for cables: A is the cross-section **area**, not the diameter.
