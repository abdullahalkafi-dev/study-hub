### The checklist for sketching a curve y = f(x)
1. **Domain.** Where is f defined?
2. **Intercepts** (and symmetry: even f(−x) = f(x), odd f(−x) = −f(x)).
3. **Asymptotes.** Vertical (denominator zero), horizontal (limit as x → ±∞), slant (when degree top = degree bottom + 1).
4. **First derivative.** Where f′ > 0 (rising) and f′ < 0 (falling). Local maxima and minima.
5. **Second derivative.** Where f″ > 0 (concave up) and f″ < 0 (concave down). Inflection points.
6. **Plot** the key points and join them smoothly.

EX: A rational curve, f(x) = x ÷ (x² + 1)
1. Domain: all real numbers (the denominator is never zero). f is odd, so the graph has point symmetry about the origin. Intercept: (0, 0).
2. Horizontal asymptote: as x → ±∞, f → 0 (degree 1 over degree 2). So y = 0.
3. f′ = (1 − x²) ÷ (x² + 1)². Zero at x = ±1. Positive for −1 < x < 1 (rising), negative outside (falling). So **a local minimum at (−1, −1/2)** and **a local maximum at (1, 1/2)**.
4. f″ = 2x (x² − 3) ÷ (x² + 1)³. Zero at x = 0 and x = ±√3. Signs change at each, so these are **inflection points**: (0, 0), (±1.73, ±0.43).
/EX

FUNC: The curve y = x ÷ (x² + 1) | x | y | x/(x**2+1) | -6,6 | -0.7,0.7 | - | A peak at x = 1 with height 1/2, a trough at x = −1, and a flattening tail toward zero (the horizontal asymptote). The curve is odd: rotate it by 180° about the origin and it looks the same.

EX: A curve with a slant asymptote, f(x) = x² ÷ (x − 1)
1. Domain: x ≠ 1. Vertical asymptote x = 1. Intercept: (0, 0).
2. Long division: x² ÷ (x − 1) = x + 1 + 1 ÷ (x − 1). For large |x| the curve approaches the line **y = x + 1**, the slant asymptote.
3. f′ = (x² − 2x) ÷ (x − 1)² = x (x − 2) ÷ (x − 1)². Zero at x = 0 and x = 2. At x = 0 the slope changes from positive to negative: **local maximum (0, 0)**. At x = 2: **local minimum (2, 4)**.
4. f″ = 2 ÷ (x − 1)³: positive for x > 1 (concave up), negative for x < 1 (concave down). There is no inflection point since x = 1 is not in the domain.
/EX

FUNC: Slant asymptote | x | y | x**2/(x-1) ;; x+1 | -4,5 | -10,12 | - | The curve y = x² ÷ (x − 1) (teal) hugs the vertical line x = 1 and the slanted line y = x + 1 (orange). Its local maximum is at the origin and its local minimum at (2, 4).

### More related rates
EX: A shadow
1. A lamp is 5 m high. A man 1.8 m tall walks away at 1.2 m/s. How fast does the tip of his shadow move, and how fast does the shadow lengthen?
2. Let x be his distance from the lamp and L the shadow's length. Similar triangles: L ÷ 1.8 = (x + L) ÷ 5, so 3.2 L = 1.8 x and L = 0.5625 x.
3. The tip is at x + L = 1.5625 x. Its speed: 1.5625 × 1.2 = **1.875 m/s**. The shadow lengthens at 0.5625 × 1.2 = **0.675 m/s**.
4. In general: tip speed = v H ÷ (H − h), shadow growth = v h ÷ (H − h).
/EX

LAB: The lengthening shadow | Choose the lamp height, the person's height and their walking speed. The graph shows the shadow length growing steadily with time. | H:Lamp height (m):3:10:0.5:5 ;; h:Person height (m):1:2:0.1:1.8 ;; v:Walking speed (m/s):0.5:3:0.1:1.2 | v*H/(H-h):Speed of the shadow tip (m/s):3 ;; v*h/(H-h):Shadow lengthening rate (m/s):3 ;; v*10*h/(H-h):Shadow length after 10 s (m):2 | v*x*h/(H-h) | 0,10 | 0,15

EX: A conical tank
1. A conical tank (point down) has radius 2 m and height 4 m at the top. Water flows in at 3 m³/min. How fast does the water level rise when the depth is 2 m?
2. At depth h the radius is r = h/2 (similar triangles), so V = (1/3) π (h/2)² h = π h³ ÷ 12.
3. dV/dt = π h² ÷ 4 × dh/dt. At h = 2: 3 = π × (4/4) × dh/dt, so dh/dt = 3 ÷ π = **0.955 m/min**.
4. At greater depth, the surface is wider, so the level rises more slowly.
/EX

EX: Two cars
1. Car A drives east at 60 km/h, and car B drives south at 80 km/h, both away from an intersection. How fast is the distance between them growing when A is 3 km and B is 4 km from it?
2. z² = x² + y². Differentiate: 2z z′ = 2x x′ + 2y y′. At x = 3, y = 4, z = 5.
3. z′ = (3 × 60 + 4 × 80) ÷ 5 = **100 km/h**.
/EX

TRAP: In a sketch, a point where f′ = 0 is not necessarily a maximum or minimum (the x³ curve has a flat inflection), and f″ = 0 does not always mean an inflection (x⁴ at 0): you need a **sign change**. In related rates, relate the variables with an equation **before** you differentiate.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: centre of gravity and tipping | 35 min

### Where does a body's weight act?
For ordinary-sized objects, gravity pulls on every bit of mass, and the total weight acts as if it were at one point, the **centre of gravity** (which equals the centre of mass in a uniform gravitational field). For a uniform symmetric body, it is at the geometric centre.

### Tipping rule
A body on a flat surface stays upright as long as the **vertical line through its centre of gravity falls inside its base of support**. If it falls outside, the weight's torque about the edge of the base tips the body over.

EQ: stable while the CG is above the base; it tips about an edge when the torque of the push exceeds the weight's restoring torque about that edge

EX: A box on a slope
1. A uniform box has width w = 0.4 m and height H = 1.0 m, standing on a ramp that tilts slowly. When the tilt angle θ is such that the CG is directly above the lower edge, the box tips.
2. The CG is at height H/2 = 0.5 m above the base and w/2 = 0.2 m from the lower edge. It is above the edge when tan θ = (w/2) ÷ (H/2) = w/H = 0.4.
3. **θ_tip = tan⁻¹(0.4) = 21.8°**. It slides first if μ < tan θ_tip = 0.4, and tips first if μ is greater.
/EX

EX: The tipping shelf (this week's story)
1. A bookshelf is 1.8 m tall and 0.30 m deep, with a mass of 40 kg. Its centre of gravity is 0.9 m up and 0.15 m behind the front edge.
2. A child pulls on a loaded drawer so that it sticks out in front. A load of m kg sits 0.4 m in front of the front edge (the tipping axis).
3. Restoring torque about the front edge: 40g × 0.15 = 6g. Tipping torque: m g × 0.4.
4. Tipping starts when 0.4 m = 6, so **m = 15 kg**. A fuller drawer (more than 15 kg) tips the shelf forward. That is why shelves and wardrobes should be fixed to the wall, and why heavy items go on the lowest shelf: a low CG gives a larger angle before tipping.
5. A force pulling at the top: a horizontal pull F at 1.8 m height tips it when F × 1.8 = 40g × 0.15, so F = **32.7 N**, about the weight of a 3.3 kg object. A small force at the top is enough.
/EX

EX: A truck on a bend
1. A truck has a track width of 2 m and its CG is 1.6 m high. On a flat circular bend of radius 80 m, the friction force that supplies the centripetal force acts at ground level, and the truck tips (about the outer wheels) when the horizontal acceleration a satisfies a × 1.6 = g × 1.0, so a_tip = g × 0.625.
2. v² ÷ r = 0.625 g gives v = √(0.625 × 9.8 × 80) = √490 = **22.1 m/s** (about 80 km/h).
3. A car with a low CG (say 0.5 m) has a_tip = 2 g, so it slides (limited by μ) long before it tips. **Tall loaded trucks and SUVs are the vehicles that roll over.**
/EX

EX: Leaning towers
1. A uniform tower of width w = 15 m and height 56 m (like the tower of Pisa, roughly) tips when it tilts to tan θ = w/H = 0.268, so θ = **15°**.
2. The real tower leans about 4°: it is still well inside the limit, though its foundation is under stress.
/EX

### Three kinds of equilibrium (again, with energy)
- **Stable:** after a small tilt, the CG rises, so gravity restores the body. A cone on its base, a ball in a bowl.
- **Unstable:** a small tilt lowers the CG, so the body falls over. A pencil on its tip.
- **Neutral:** the CG stays at the same height. A ball on a flat table, a cone on its side.

This is the same classification as the energy diagram of Week 5: the height of the CG is the potential energy U = m g h.

TRAP: When checking for tipping, take torques about the **edge of the base** that the body would rotate around, not about the CG. The normal force acts at that edge at the moment of tipping, so it has zero torque. Also, a wider base **and** a lower CG both help: what matters is the ratio of the half-width to the CG height.
