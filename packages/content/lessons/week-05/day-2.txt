@section math | 2 · Math: computing limits | 60 min

### The limit laws
If lim f(x) = L and lim g(x) = M as x → a, then:

EQ: lim (f + g) = L + M     lim (f g) = L M     lim (f ÷ g) = L ÷ M (if M ≠ 0)     lim (f(x))ⁿ = Lⁿ

These say limits behave nicely with arithmetic. A consequence is **direct substitution**: for polynomials, and for rational functions where the denominator is not zero at a, the limit is just f(a). The interesting case is when substitution gives **0 ÷ 0**. That means "there is more work to do", not "there is no answer".

### Technique 1: factor and cancel
EX: Cancel a common factor
1. lim (x → 3) (x² − 9) ÷ (x² − x − 6). Substitution gives 0 ÷ 0.
2. Factor: (x − 3)(x + 3) ÷ ((x − 3)(x + 2)) = (x + 3) ÷ (x + 2) for x ≠ 3.
3. Now substitute: 6 ÷ 5 = **6/5**.
/EX

### Technique 2: multiply by the conjugate
When a square root is involved, multiply top and bottom by the **conjugate** (the same expression with the opposite sign in the middle). Then (A − B)(A + B) = A² − B² removes the root.

EX: Rationalise
1. lim (x → 4) (√x − 2) ÷ (x − 4). Substitution gives 0 ÷ 0.
2. Multiply by (√x + 2) ÷ (√x + 2): numerator x − 4, so the fraction becomes (x − 4) ÷ ((x − 4)(√x + 2)) = 1 ÷ (√x + 2).
3. The limit is **1/4**.
/EX

### Technique 3: simplify a fraction within a fraction
EX: Common denominator
1. lim (x → 0) (1 ÷ (x + 2) − 1/2) ÷ x. Substitution gives 0 ÷ 0.
2. Top: (2 − (x + 2)) ÷ (2 (x + 2)) = −x ÷ (2 (x + 2)).
3. Divide by x: −1 ÷ (2 (x + 2)). As x → 0 the limit is **−1/4**.
/EX

### The difference quotient (a preview of Thursday)
EX: A limit you will use constantly
1. For a fixed x, find lim (h → 0) ((x + h)² − x²) ÷ h.
2. Expand: (x² + 2xh + h² − x²) ÷ h = (2xh + h²) ÷ h = 2x + h.
3. As h → 0, we get **2x**. This is the derivative of x², obtained as a limit.
/EX

### Technique 4: trigonometric limits
You know lim (x → 0) sin x ÷ x = 1. From it, we get many others.

EX: Using sin x ÷ x
1. lim (x → 0) sin 3x ÷ x: write it as 3 × sin 3x ÷ (3x). With u = 3x → 0, sin u ÷ u → 1, so the limit is **3**.
2. lim (x → 0) sin 5x ÷ sin 2x = (sin 5x ÷ 5x) × (2x ÷ sin 2x) × (5x ÷ 2x) → 1 × 1 × 5/2 = **5/2**.
3. lim (x → 0) tan x ÷ x = (sin x ÷ x) × (1 ÷ cos x) → 1 × 1 = **1**.
4. lim (x → 0) (1 − cos x) ÷ x². Multiply by (1 + cos x): (1 − cos²x) ÷ (x² (1 + cos x)) = (sin x ÷ x)² × 1 ÷ (1 + cos x) → 1 × 1/2 = **1/2**.
/EX

BN: 0 ÷ 0 পেলে বুঝতে হবে আরও কাজ বাকি, উত্তর নেই তা নয়। কৌশল: (১) উৎপাদকে ভেঙে কাটো, (২) বর্গমূল থাকলে অনুবন্ধী (conjugate) দিয়ে গুণ করো, (৩) ভগ্নাংশের ভেতর ভগ্নাংশ থাকলে সাধারণ হর করো, (৪) ত্রিকোণমিতিতে sin x/x → 1 ব্যবহার করো। 1 − cos x ≈ x²/2 মনে রাখলে অনেক কাজ সহজ হয়।

### Technique 5: infinite limits and vertical asymptotes
If the denominator goes to 0 while the numerator does not, the fraction blows up. The sign depends on the side.

EX: Vertical asymptote
1. f(x) = 1 ÷ (x − 2). As x → 2⁺, the values are large and positive (+∞). As x → 2⁻ they are large and negative (−∞).
2. So x = 2 is a **vertical asymptote**, and the two-sided limit does not exist.
3. For g(x) = 1 ÷ (x − 2)², both sides give +∞, so we can write lim g = ∞.
/EX

### A limit that defines e
EX: Compound interest taken to the extreme
1. Invest 1 at 100% a year. With n compounding periods, the year-end value is (1 + 1/n)ⁿ.
2. n = 1: 2. n = 10: 2.5937. n = 100: 2.7048. n = 1000: 2.7169. n = 1,000,000: 2.71828.
3. The values do not grow forever. They approach a number: **e = 2.71828…** This is where e comes from, and it is the base of the natural logarithm that appeared in Week 2.
/EX

### Limits at infinity with roots
EX: A tricky difference
1. lim (x → ∞) (√(x² + x) − x). This looks like ∞ − ∞.
2. Multiply by the conjugate: ((x² + x) − x²) ÷ (√(x² + x) + x) = x ÷ (√(x² + x) + x).
3. Divide top and bottom by x: 1 ÷ (√(1 + 1/x) + 1) → 1 ÷ 2 = **1/2**.
/EX

TRAP: Do not cancel terms across a sum. For (x + 3) ÷ (x + 5), you cannot cancel "x". Cancel only common **factors**. And do not write "0 ÷ 0 = 1": it is an indeterminate form, which means the answer could be anything and must be worked out.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: the work-energy theorem at work | 35 min

### The method
1. Pick the start and the end.
2. List **every** force that does work along the path (include friction, which is negative).
3. W_net = ½ m v² − ½ m u². Solve for what you need.

Forces perpendicular to the motion (normal force on a flat track, centripetal force) do no work and can be ignored.

### Work by gravity depends only on height
Sliding down a slope of length d and angle θ, gravity's component along the slope is m g sin θ, so W = m g sin θ × d = m g (d sin θ) = **m g h**, where h is the vertical drop. For any path (steep, gentle or curved), the work by gravity equals m g × (vertical drop). This is why a **smooth ramp of any shape gives the same speed at the bottom**.

EX: Down a frictionless ramp
1. A block is released from rest at a height of 2 m on a smooth ramp. W_gravity = m g h = ½ m v².
2. **v = √(2 g h) = √39.2 = 6.26 m/s**, whatever the shape of the ramp, and the mass cancels.
/EX

EX: The roller coaster
1. A carriage starts nearly at rest at the top of a 40 m drop on a track with negligible friction.
2. v = √(2 × 9.8 × 40) = √784 = **28 m/s**, about 100 km/h.
3. The radius of the curves at the bottom matters for the force, but not for the speed.
/EX

BN: ঢাল বেয়ে নামলে মহাকর্ষের কাজ = mgh, যেখানে h হলো খাড়া নামার উচ্চতা। পথ যেমনই হোক (খাড়া, ঢালু বা বাঁকা), কাজ একই। তাই ঘর্ষণহীন যেকোনো পথে নিচে পৌঁছানোর গতি v = √(2gh), ভর কাটা যায়।

EX: Ramp with friction
1. A 5 kg block slides 4 m down a 30° slope, starting at rest. μ_k = 0.2 and g = 9.8.
2. Height dropped: 4 × sin 30° = 2 m, so W_gravity = 5 × 9.8 × 2 = 98 J.
3. N = m g cos 30° = 42.4 N, friction = 0.2 × 42.4 = 8.49 N, so W_friction = −8.49 × 4 = **−33.9 J**.
4. W_net = 98 − 33.9 = 64.1 J = ½ × 5 × v², so v² = 25.6 and **v = 5.06 m/s**. Compare with 6.26 m/s without friction.
/EX

EX: Pushing a crate
1. A 30 kg crate is pushed 8 m along a floor with a 100 N force. μ_k = 0.25.
2. W_push = 800 J. Friction = 0.25 × 294 = 73.5 N, so W_friction = −588 J.
3. W_net = 212 J. From rest: v = √(2 × 212 ÷ 30) = √14.1 = **3.76 m/s**.
/EX

### Springs and variable forces
The work to compress or stretch a spring by x is the area of a triangle:

EQ: W_spring = ½ k x²

When the spring pushes an object back to its natural length, it can give all of that work to the object.

EX: A spring gun
1. A spring with k = 500 N/m is compressed 8 cm and launches a 0.2 kg block along a smooth table.
2. Work = ½ × 500 × 0.0064 = **1.6 J**.
3. ½ × 0.2 × v² = 1.6, so v² = 16 and **v = 4 m/s**.
/EX

### Work from a graph
EX: Piecewise force
1. A force rises uniformly from 0 to 20 N over the first 2 m, stays at 20 N for the next 3 m, then drops uniformly to 0 over the last 1 m.
2. Areas: ½ × 2 × 20 = 20 J, then 3 × 20 = 60 J, then ½ × 1 × 20 = 10 J.
3. Total work = **90 J**.
/EX

TRAP: Do not forget **friction's negative work**, and do not count the normal force or a centripetal force as doing work. Also, "m g h" uses the **vertical** drop, not the distance along the slope.

### Practice
LVL: Level 1 | Direct
P: A 2 kg stone falls from rest through 20 m (no air resistance). Find the speed. => √(2 × 9.8 × 20) = **19.8 m/s**.
P: A spring with k = 300 N/m is stretched 5 cm. How much work was done? => ½ × 300 × 0.0025 = **0.375 J**.
LVL: Level 2 | Apply
P: A 0.1 kg ball is thrown upward at 15 m/s. How high does it go (use the work-energy method)? => −m g h = −½ m v², so h = 225 ÷ 19.6 = **11.5 m**.
P: A sled of mass 20 kg slides down a 15 m long slope inclined at 20° starting from rest, with μ_k = 0.1. Find the speed at the bottom. => Height = 15 × sin 20° = 5.13 m. Gravity work = 20 × 9.8 × 5.13 = 1005.5 J. Friction = 0.1 × 20 × 9.8 × cos 20° × 15 = 276.3 J. Net = 729.2 J, so v = √(2 × 729.2 ÷ 20) = √72.9 = **8.54 m/s**.
P: A 1500 kg car going at 20 m/s brakes to a stop in 40 m. Find the average braking force. => KE = ½ × 1500 × 400 = 300,000 J. F = 300,000 ÷ 40 = **7500 N**.
LVL: Challenge | The loop-the-loop
P: A cart is released from rest at height h on a frictionless track that leads into a vertical loop of radius r. What is the smallest h that keeps the cart on the track at the top of the loop? Evaluate for r = 5 m. => At the top the least speed (from Week 4) gives v² = g r. Energy: m g (h − 2r) = ½ m v² = ½ m g r, so h − 2r = r/2 and **h = 2.5 r**. For r = 5 m, h = **12.5 m**. Releasing from a lower height means the cart drops off the track before reaching the top.
