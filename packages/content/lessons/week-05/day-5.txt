@section math | 2 · Math: derivatives of sin x, cos x and eˣ | 60 min

### The derivative of sin x
Start from the definition and use the angle-addition formula from Week 3:

(sin(x + h) − sin x) ÷ h = (sin x cos h + cos x sin h − sin x) ÷ h = sin x · (cos h − 1) ÷ h + cos x · (sin h ÷ h).

Now take h → 0. We know sin h ÷ h → 1. And (cos h − 1) ÷ h = −h · (1 − cos h) ÷ h² → −0 × 1/2 = 0, using the limit (1 − cos h) ÷ h² → 1/2 from Tuesday.

So the first part goes to sin x × 0 = 0 and the second to cos x × 1:

EQ: d/dx (sin x) = cos x     d/dx (cos x) = −sin x     (x in radians)

For cosine, cos(x + h) − cos x = cos x (cos h − 1) − sin x sin h gives, in the same way, −sin x.

BN: sin x এর অন্তরকলজ cos x, আর cos x এর অন্তরকলজ −sin x (শুধু রেডিয়ানে)। প্রমাণে লাগে sin(x + h) এর সূত্র আর দুটি লিমিট: sin h/h → 1 এবং (cos h − 1)/h → 0।

**See it on the graph.** Where sine climbs fastest (at x = 0) its slope is 1, which is the peak value of cosine. At the top of the sine wave the slope is 0, where cosine crosses zero. Where sine falls fastest (at π) the slope is −1, the bottom of cosine. The graph of the derivative is the graph of the slope.

FUNC: The slope of sine is cosine | x (rad) | y | sin(x) ;; cos(x) | 0,2*pi | -1.5,1.5 | pi | Teal: y = sin x. Orange: its slope function, y = cos x. When the teal curve is steepest, the orange curve is at its highest or lowest. When the teal curve is flat (at a peak or a trough), the orange curve is zero.

The derivatives cycle with period four: sin → cos → −sin → −cos → sin.

EX: Using the rules
1. d/dx (3 sin x − 2 cos x) = 3 cos x + 2 sin x.
2. The tangent to y = sin x at x = π/3: slope = cos(π/3) = 1/2, and the point is (π/3, √3/2). So y − √3/2 = (1/2)(x − π/3).
3. Where is y = sin x + cos x flat? The slope cos x − sin x = 0 gives tan x = 1, so x = π/4 or 5π/4, where the values are **√2** and **−√2**. This agrees with the form √2 sin(x + π/4) from Week 3.
/EX

### The derivative of eˣ
(e^(x+h) − eˣ) ÷ h = eˣ · (eʰ − 1) ÷ h.

The number e is chosen so that (eʰ − 1) ÷ h → 1 as h → 0. Check by table: h = 0.1 gives 1.0517, h = 0.01 gives 1.00502, h = 0.001 gives 1.0005. So:

EQ: d/dx (eˣ) = eˣ

The function eˣ is **its own derivative**. Its slope at every point equals its height. No other function (apart from multiples of it) does this, and that is why e is the natural base for growth.

On Monday, we found (2ʰ − 1) ÷ h → 0.693 = ln 2. The same argument gives **d/dx (2ˣ) = (ln 2) 2ˣ**, and for any base a: **d/dx (aˣ) = (ln a) aˣ**. Only for a = e is the constant equal to 1.

EX: A constant in the exponent
1. For eᵏˣ: (eᵏ⁽ˣ⁺ʰ⁾ − eᵏˣ) ÷ h = eᵏˣ · (eᵏʰ − 1) ÷ h = k eᵏˣ · (eᵏʰ − 1) ÷ (kh).
2. As h → 0, kh → 0 and the last factor → 1. So **d/dx (eᵏˣ) = k eᵏˣ**.
3. This is the law of growth and decay: a quantity whose rate of change is proportional to itself.
/EX

EX: Bacteria growth
1. A culture has N(t) = 500 e^(0.3t) bacteria after t hours.
2. dN/dt = 0.3 × 500 e^(0.3t) = 150 e^(0.3t) = 0.3 N.
3. At t = 0 the growth rate is **150 per hour**. At t = 5: N = 500 e^1.5 = 2241 and the rate is 0.3 × 2241 = **672 per hour**. The bigger the culture, the faster it grows.
/EX

EX: Cooling
1. A cup of tea cools as T(t) = 20 + 60 e^(−0.1t) °C. Then dT/dt = −6 e^(−0.1t).
2. At t = 0: **−6 °C per minute**. At t = 10 min: −6 e⁻¹ = **−2.2 °C per minute**. The tea cools quickly at first and slowly later. The room temperature 20 °C is the limit as t → ∞.
/EX

### Oscillation: a look ahead
EX: A vibrating mass
1. A mass on a spring moves as x(t) = 0.05 sin(10t) metres.
2. Velocity v = x′ = 0.5 cos(10t) m/s (the factor 10 comes out, just as k came out of eᵏˣ). The maximum speed is **0.5 m/s**.
3. Acceleration a = v′ = −5 sin(10t) = −100 x. The maximum is **5 m/s²**.
4. Notice a = −100 x: the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and opposite to it. This is the signature of simple harmonic motion, the subject of Week 9.
/EX

### The second derivative
The derivative of the derivative is the **second derivative**, written f″(x) or d²y/dx². If f is position, f″ is acceleration. For a graph, f″ > 0 means the slope is increasing (the curve bends upward, like a cup), and f″ < 0 means the curve bends downward.

EX: A second derivative
1. f(x) = x³ − 3x. f′ = 3x² − 3 and f″ = **6x**.
2. At x = 1, f″ = 6 > 0, so the curve bends upward and the flat point is a **minimum**. At x = −1, f″ = −6 < 0, so it is a **maximum**.
/EX

TRAP: The formulas d/dx(sin x) = cos x hold **only in radians**. In degrees there is an extra factor of π/180. Also, do not confuse eˣ (variable exponent) with xⁿ (variable base): the power rule does **not** apply to eˣ, which does **not** give x eˣ⁻¹.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: force from potential energy, and energy diagrams | 35 min

### The link between force and energy
Last time you learned that the work done by a conservative force equals −ΔU. Take a tiny displacement dx: F dx = −dU. Divide by dx:

EQ: F = − dU/dx

**The force is minus the slope of the potential-energy curve.** The system is pushed "downhill" on the graph of U.

EX: Three familiar cases
1. Spring: U = ½ k x², so F = −k x. ✓ (Hooke's law, restoring.)
2. Gravity near Earth: U = m g h, so F = −m g. ✓ (Downward.)
3. Gravity in space: U = −G M m ÷ r. Since d/dr(−G M m r⁻¹) = G M m r⁻², F = **−G M m ÷ r²** ✓ (Newton's law of gravitation, attractive, toward smaller r.)
/EX

BN: বল = −dU/dx: স্থিতিশক্তির লেখচিত্রের ঢালের ঋণাত্মক মান। স্প্রিং: U = ½kx² থেকে F = −kx। অভিকর্ষ: U = mgh থেকে F = −mg। মহাকর্ষ: U = −GMm/r থেকে F = −GMm/r²। বস্তু লেখচিত্রের "ঢাল বেয়ে নিচে" ঠেলা খায়।

### Reading an energy diagram
Plot U against position x.
- **Equilibrium** where dU/dx = 0 (flat slope, zero force).
- **Stable** equilibrium at a minimum of U: a small push makes the force restore the object, like a marble in a bowl.
- **Unstable** equilibrium at a maximum: a small push makes it roll away, like a marble on top of a hill.
- **Turning points:** the total energy E is a horizontal line. Where E = U, the kinetic energy is zero, so the object stops and turns back. It cannot go where U > E, since KE = E − U would be negative.

FUNC: A potential energy curve | position x (m) | U (J) | x**3-3*x | -2.2,2.2 | -4,4 | - | U = x³ − 3x. It has a peak at x = −1 (unstable equilibrium) and a dip at x = 1 (stable equilibrium).

EX: Reading U = x³ − 3x
1. F = −dU/dx = −(3x² − 3) = 3 − 3x². Equilibrium where 3 − 3x² = 0: **x = ±1**.
2. At x = 1: U = −2, a minimum (U″ = 6 > 0), so it is **stable**. At x = −1: U = 2, a maximum, so it is **unstable**.
3. Test: at x = 1.1, F = 3 − 3.63 = −0.63 N (pushes back toward smaller x). At x = 0.9, F = 3 − 2.43 = +0.57 N (pushes toward larger x). Both push toward x = 1 ✓.
/EX

EX: The spring as an energy well
1. A 0.2 kg mass on a spring with k = 100 N/m has total energy E = 0.5 J.
2. Turning points: ½ k x² = 0.5, so x² = 0.01 and **x = ±0.1 m**.
3. At the centre (x = 0): all energy is kinetic, ½ m v² = 0.5, so v = √5 = **2.24 m/s**, the maximum speed.
4. This back-and-forth motion in a well is the simple harmonic oscillation (Week 9).
/EX

EX: The roller coaster as an energy diagram
1. The height profile is the potential energy curve U = m g h. A car released from rest at the highest hill has E = m g h_max.
2. At any point, KE = E − m g h, so the car moves fastest at the lowest points and slows on the hills. If a later hill is taller than the first, it can never get over it (U > E there).
/EX

### A mixed workout
EX: Ramp, rough floor, spring
1. A 2 kg block is released from rest at a height of 1.5 m on a smooth ramp. It then crosses 3 m of rough floor (μ = 0.3) and hits a spring with k = 200 N/m on a smooth floor. How far does the spring compress?
2. Energy at the start: m g h = 2 × 9.8 × 1.5 = 29.4 J.
3. Friction on the floor: μ m g × 3 = 0.3 × 19.6 × 3 = 17.64 J removed.
4. Left for the spring: 29.4 − 17.64 = 11.76 J = ½ × 200 × x². So x² = 0.1176 and **x = 0.343 m**.
/EX

TRAP: A zero force does not always mean the object is at rest: it only means the slope of U is zero. Also, "stable" and "unstable" are about **small displacements**: a marble at the top of a hill is in equilibrium, but any nudge sends it away.

LVL: Challenge | The Lennard-Jones taste
P: The potential energy between two atoms is approximated by U(r) = 1/r¹² − 2/r⁶ (in suitable units). Find the separation where the force is zero and the energy there. Is it stable? => U′ = −12/r¹³ + 12/r⁷ = 12 (r⁶ − 1) ÷ r¹³ = 0, so **r = 1**. U(1) = 1 − 2 = **−1**. For r slightly below 1, U′ < 0 (force pushes outward, repulsive); for r slightly above 1, U′ > 0 (force pulls inward, attractive). So it is a **stable minimum**: atoms settle at this bonding distance.
