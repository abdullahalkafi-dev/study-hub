### 1 · Fibonacci and the golden ratio
The Fibonacci numbers 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, … obey F_(n+1) = F_n + F_(n−1). In matrix form:

EQ: [[1, 1], [1, 0]]ⁿ = [[F_(n+1), F_n], [F_n, F_(n−1)]]

EX: Eigenvalues of the Fibonacci matrix
1. F = [[1, 1], [1, 0]]: trace 1, det −1, so λ² − λ − 1 = 0 and **λ = φ = (1 + √5) ÷ 2 = 1.618** (the golden ratio) and **ψ = (1 − √5) ÷ 2 = −0.618**.
2. Check an eigenvector: F (φ, 1) = (φ + 1, φ) = (φ², φ) = φ (φ, 1), since φ² = φ + 1 ✓.
3. Diagonalising gives **Binet's formula: F_n = (φⁿ − ψⁿ) ÷ √5**. For n = 10: φ¹⁰ = 122.99, ψ¹⁰ = 0.0081, so F₁₀ = 122.98 ÷ 2.236 = **55** ✓.
4. Because |ψ| < 1, F_n is the integer nearest to φⁿ ÷ √5. For n = 50: **12,586,269,025**.
5. The ratio F_(n+1) ÷ F_n tends to φ: 6765 ÷ 4181 = 1.61803.
6. Taking determinants: det (Fⁿ) = (−1)ⁿ gives **F_(n+1) F_(n−1) − F_n² = (−1)ⁿ**. For n = 10: 89 × 34 − 55² = 3026 − 3025 = 1 ✓.
/EX

### 2 · Populations that grow in stages
A population has juveniles J and adults A. Each adult produces b juveniles per step; a fraction s_j of the juveniles become adults; a fraction s_a of the adults survive. Then

(J, A)_next = L (J, A), with the **Leslie matrix** L = [[0, b], [s_j, s_a]].

EX: A growing population
1. b = 2, s_j = 0.5, s_a = 0.8: L = [[0, 2], [0.5, 0.8]]. The trace is 0.8 and the determinant is −1, so λ² − 0.8λ − 1 = 0 and **λ = (0.8 + √4.64) ÷ 2 = 1.477**.
2. The dominant eigenvalue is the long-run growth factor: the population grows **47.7% per step**.
3. The eigenvector (A − λI) v = 0: −1.477 J + 2 A = 0, so v = (2, 1.477): juveniles and adults in the ratio **1.354 : 1**.
4. Start from (10, 10): the next steps are (20, 13), (26, 20.4), (40.8, 29.3), (58.6, 43.9), (87.7, 64.4). The total grows by factors 1.41, 1.51, 1.46, 1.48, … tending to **1.477**.
5. **Replacement level:** the population is stable when λ = 1, i.e. det (L − I) = 0: (−1)(s_a − 1) − b s_j = 0, so **b = (1 − s_a) ÷ s_j = 0.2 ÷ 0.5 = 0.4**. Fewer than 0.4 juveniles per adult per step and the population dies out.
/EX

### 3 · Two coupled oscillators: normal modes
Two equal masses m are each attached to a wall by a spring k and to each other by a spring k_c. Newton's second law for the displacements x₁, x₂:

m x₁″ = −k x₁ − k_c (x₁ − x₂)     m x₂″ = −k x₂ − k_c (x₂ − x₁)

In matrix form x″ = −M x, with M = (1 ÷ m) [[k + k_c, −k_c], [−k_c, k + k_c]]. Try x = v cos ωt: then M v = ω² v, so **the eigenvalues of M are ω²** and the eigenvectors are the **normal modes**, in which everything oscillates at one frequency.

EX: Normal modes
1. **In-phase mode** v = (1, 1): M (1, 1) = (k ÷ m)(1, 1), so ω₁² = k ÷ m. The middle spring is not stretched.
2. **Opposite mode** v = (1, −1): M (1, −1) = ((k + 2k_c) ÷ m)(1, −1), so ω₂² = (k + 2k_c) ÷ m. The middle spring is stretched twice as much, so it oscillates faster.
3. With m = 1 kg, k = 100 N/m and k_c = 20 N/m: **ω₁ = 10 rad/s** (f₁ = 1.592 Hz) and **ω₂ = √140 = 11.83 rad/s** (f₂ = 1.883 Hz).
4. Pull only mass 1 and release: x = ½ (in-phase mode) + ½ (opposite mode). The two frequencies **beat** at f₂ − f₁ = 0.292 Hz, and the energy swaps between the masses every **3.43 s**, just like the beats you met in Week 10.
/EX

TRAP: In Binet's formula do not drop the ψⁿ term for small n (it is tiny only for large n). In the Leslie model the **dominant** eigenvalue (the largest in magnitude) governs the long run. And in the coupled oscillator, eigenvalues of M are ω² (squares of the angular frequencies): take the square root.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: entropy | 35 min

### A quantity that always grows
Clausius defined entropy through heat exchanged **reversibly** at temperature T:

EQ: dS = δQ_rev ÷ T     so at constant temperature     ΔS = Q ÷ T     (unit J/K)

Entropy is a **state function**, like energy: it depends only on the state, not on how you got there. The second law, in its entropy form:

EQ: ΔS_total = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings ≥ 0     (= 0 only for reversible processes)

BN: এনট্রপি: dS = δQ_rev/T, ধ্রুব তাপমাত্রায় ΔS = Q/T (J/K)। এটা অবস্থার ফাংশন। দ্বিতীয় সূত্র: ΔS_মোট = ΔS_ব্যবস্থা + ΔS_পরিবেশ ≥ 0; পরিবর্তনীয় প্রক্রিয়ায় সমান, অপরিবর্তনীয় প্রক্রিয়ায় বড়। তাপ গরম থেকে ঠান্ডায় গেলে মোট এনট্রপি বাড়ে।

EX: Heat flowing from hot to cold
1. 1000 J flows from a hot reservoir at 400 K to a cold one at 300 K. The hot one loses entropy: ΔS_H = −1000 ÷ 400 = **−2.50 J/K**. The cold one gains ΔS_C = +1000 ÷ 300 = **+3.33 J/K**.
2. Total: **+0.83 J/K > 0**. The reverse flow would give −0.83 J/K, forbidden by the second law.
3. **Lost work:** if instead of conducting, the heat ran a Carnot engine between 400 K and 300 K, the work would be 1000 × (1 − 300 ÷ 400) = 250 J. Direct conduction wastes this 250 J, and exactly **T_C ΔS_total = 300 × 0.833 = 250 J**. Entropy production measures wasted work.
/EX

### Entropy changes in common processes
EX: Calculating ΔS
1. **Melting ice** at 273.15 K: 1 kg takes 334 kJ, so ΔS = 334,000 ÷ 273.15 = **1223 J/K**. **Boiling** 1 kg of water at 373.15 K: 2.26 × 10⁶ ÷ 373.15 = **6057 J/K**.
2. **Heating water:** dS = m c dT ÷ T, so ΔS = m c ln (T₂ ÷ T₁). For 1 kg from 20 °C to 80 °C: 4186 × ln (353.15 ÷ 293.15) = **779 J/K**.
3. **Mixing:** 1 kg of water at 80 °C and 1 kg at 20 °C reach 50 °C (323.15 K). ΔS = 4186 × [ln (323.15 ÷ 353.15) + ln (323.15 ÷ 293.15)] = 4186 × (−0.0888 + 0.0974) = **+36.2 J/K**.
4. **Ideal gas:** ΔS = n C_V ln (T₂ ÷ T₁) + n R ln (V₂ ÷ V₁). In a **free expansion** to twice the volume, nothing exchanges heat but ΔS = n R ln 2 = **5.76 J/K per mole** (entropy is a state function, so we compute it along any reversible path between the same states).
5. **An ice cube** of 20 g melts in a 293 K room: the ice gains 0.02 × 334,000 ÷ 273.15 = 24.46 J/K and the room loses 6680 ÷ 293.15 = 22.79 J/K, a total of **+1.67 J/K**.
6. **A reversible isothermal expansion** has ΔS_gas = n R ln 2 and ΔS_surroundings = −n R ln 2: total **0**. The free expansion has the same ΔS_gas but ΔS_surroundings = 0: total **+n R ln 2**.

### Entropy and the Carnot cycle
For a Carnot engine, Q_H ÷ T_H = Q_C ÷ T_C: the entropy the engine takes from the hot reservoir equals the entropy it gives to the cold one, and the net entropy change is **zero** (reversible). For a real engine, Q_C ÷ T_C > Q_H ÷ T_H, so more heat must be rejected than in the Carnot case, and **e < e_Carnot**.

A single reservoir cannot drive an engine either: taking Q from one reservoir at T and turning it all into work gives ΔS = −Q ÷ T < 0, forbidden. That is the Kelvin–Planck statement derived from entropy.

TRAP: Use **kelvin** in Q ÷ T. A system's entropy can decrease (a freezing liquid), but then its surroundings gain more, so the **total** never decreases. And entropy is only equal to Q ÷ T for a **reversible** heat exchange: for an irreversible process find a reversible path between the same two states.
