# Principal components and principal axes. Then: real engines and entropy production
lead: Today the eigenvector idea does two jobs. In data science it finds the direction along which your data varies most (principal component analysis, PCA), which is how a hundred measurements are squeezed into two numbers. In mechanics it finds the axes about which a body spins most easily or most stubbornly (the inertia tensor). In physics you meet the engines in your car, with the Otto and Diesel cycles, learn why real engines reach only half of the Carnot limit, and measure the waste as entropy produced.

### The covariance matrix
Take n data points (xᵢ, yᵢ). Subtract the means to get deviations. The **covariance matrix** is

EQ: C = [[s_xx, s_xy], [s_xy, s_yy]]     with s_xx = Σ dx² ÷ (n − 1), s_yy = Σ dy² ÷ (n − 1), s_xy = Σ dx·dy ÷ (n − 1)

It is **symmetric**, so yesterday's theorem applies: real eigenvalues, perpendicular eigenvectors. The eigenvector of the **largest** eigenvalue is the **first principal component**: the direction of greatest spread. The eigenvalue is the variance along it, and the share λ₁ ÷ (λ₁ + λ₂) is the fraction of the total variance it explains.

BN: সহভেদাংক ম্যাট্রিক্স C প্রতিসম, তাই eigenvalue বাস্তব আর eigenvector লম্ব। বৃহত্তম eigenvalue এর eigenvector হলো প্রথম মুখ্য উপাদান: ডেটা সবচেয়ে বেশি ছড়ায় যে দিকে। eigenvalue = ঐ দিকের ভেদাংক; λ₁/(λ₁+λ₂) = কত অংশ ভেদাংক সে ব্যাখ্যা করে।

EX: A four-point data set
1. Data: (2, 3), (4, 5), (6, 9), (8, 9). Means: x̄ = 5, ȳ = 6.5.
2. Deviations: dx = −3, −1, 1, 3 and dy = −3.5, −1.5, 2.5, 2.5.
3. s_xx = (9 + 1 + 1 + 9) ÷ 3 = **6.667**. s_yy = (12.25 + 2.25 + 6.25 + 6.25) ÷ 3 = 27 ÷ 3 = **9**. s_xy = (10.5 + 1.5 + 2.5 + 7.5) ÷ 3 = 22 ÷ 3 = **7.333**.
4. Trace = 15.667 and det = 6.667 × 9 − 7.333² = 60 − 53.78 = 6.222. So λ² − 15.667λ + 6.222 = 0 and **λ₁ = 15.26, λ₂ = 0.408**.
5. The first principal component: (C − 15.26 I) v = 0 gives v₁ = **(0.649, 0.761)**, at an angle tan⁻¹ (0.761 ÷ 0.649) = **49.5°**. The variance explained is 15.26 ÷ 15.667 = **97.4%**.
6. The "scores" (the coordinate of each point along v₁): −3 × 0.649 − 3.5 × 0.761 = **−4.61**, then **−1.79**, **+2.55**, **+3.85**. Four points in two dimensions are reduced to four single numbers, and 97.4% of the information is kept.
/EX

### Why PCA is useful
EX: Compression and tomography of data
1. **Images and faces:** 10,000 pixel values per face. PCA finds, say, 50 directions ("eigenfaces") that capture 95% of the variation, so each face is stored as 50 numbers.
2. **Dimension reduction for machine learning:** remove the directions with tiny eigenvalues (noise) before training, which makes models faster and often better.
3. **Reading the scree:** if the eigenvalues are 15.26 and 0.41, one direction does almost everything. If they were 8 and 7.7, the cloud would be round and there would be nothing to gain.
4. **Always centre first:** subtract the means. Without that, the first "component" just points at the mean.
/EX

TRAP: PCA finds directions of **variance**, not of importance. If one column is in millimetres and another in kilometres, the large numbers dominate, so **standardise** (divide by the standard deviation) when the units differ. And the sign of an eigenvector is arbitrary: (0.649, 0.761) and (−0.649, −0.761) are the same axis.

### The inertia tensor in a plane
For point masses in the xy-plane the moment of inertia about an axis through the origin in the plane in direction n = (n_x, n_y) is Σ m (x n_y − y n_x)². This is a quadratic form nᵀ I n with the symmetric matrix

EQ: I = [[Σ m y², −Σ m x y], [−Σ m x y, Σ m x²]]

Its eigenvectors are the **principal axes**, its eigenvalues the **principal moments**.

EX: Two bodies
1. **A dumbbell on the diagonal:** 2 kg at (1, 1) and 2 kg at (−1, −1). Σ m y² = 4, Σ m x² = 4, Σ m xy = 4. So I = [[4, −4], [−4, 4]], with **λ = 8 along (1, −1)** and **λ = 0 along (1, 1)**. Check: about the axis along the dumbbell the masses are on the axis, so the moment is 0 ✓. About the perpendicular axis through the centre, each mass is √2 away: 2 × (√2)² × 2 = **8** ✓.
2. **A cross:** 1 kg at (±3, 0) and 1 kg at (0, ±1). Σ m x² = 18, Σ m y² = 2, Σ m xy = 0. I = [[2, 0], [0, 18]]: already diagonal, so the coordinate axes are the principal axes. The moment is **2** about the x-axis and **18** about the y-axis: the body is easy to spin about its long axis and hard about the short one.
3. **Why engineers care:** a body spinning about a principal axis has angular momentum parallel to its angular velocity and does not wobble. An unbalanced car wheel is one whose spin axis is not a principal axis: it shakes, and the garage adds small masses until it is.
/EX

BN: ভরের বিন্দু-সংগ্রহের জন্য I = [[Σmy², −Σmxy], [−Σmxy, Σmx²]]। eigenvector = মুখ্য অক্ষ, eigenvalue = মুখ্য জড়তা ভ্রামক। মুখ্য অক্ষে ঘুরলে বস্তু টলে না; গাড়ির চাকার ব্যালান্সিং আসলে ঘূর্ণন অক্ষকে মুখ্য অক্ষে আনা।

### The Otto cycle (petrol engine)
Four strokes idealised as: **adiabatic compression** (ratio r = V₁ ÷ V₂), **heating at constant volume** (the spark), **adiabatic expansion**, **cooling at constant volume** (exhaust). The efficiency depends only on r:

EQ: η_Otto = 1 − r^(1 − γ) = 1 − 1 ÷ r^(γ − 1)     (γ = 1.4 for air)

EX: A petrol engine
1. **r = 8:** η = 1 − 8^(−0.4) = 1 − 0.4353 = **56.5%**.
2. **r = 9:** η = 1 − 9^(−0.4) = 1 − 0.4152 = **58.5%**. **r = 10:** **60.2%**. Higher compression gives better efficiency, but too high a ratio makes the fuel ignite early ("knocking").
3. **Step by step for r = 8, T₁ = 300 K, peak T₃ = 1800 K:** T₂ = 300 × 8^0.4 = **689 K**. Heat in (per mole, C_V = 20.79 J/(mol K)) = 20.79 × (1800 − 689) = **23,090 J**. T₄ = 1800 ÷ 8^0.4 = **783.5 K**. Heat out = 20.79 × (783.5 − 300) = **10,050 J**. Work = 23,090 − 10,050 = **13,040 J**. η = 13,040 ÷ 23,090 = **56.5%** ✓ (the formula agrees).
4. **Compare with Carnot between 1800 K and 300 K:** 1 − 300 ÷ 1800 = **83.3%**. The Otto cycle is lower because it takes in heat over a range of temperatures, not all at the top one.
/EX

### The Diesel cycle
Diesel engines compress air alone (r around 18), then inject fuel that burns **at constant pressure** while the gas expands from V₂ to ρV₂ (ρ = cut-off ratio). The efficiency is

EQ: η_Diesel = 1 − (1 ÷ r^(γ − 1)) × (ρ^γ − 1) ÷ (γ (ρ − 1))

EX: A diesel engine
r = 18, ρ = 2, γ = 1.4: r^(−0.4) = 0.3147. (2^1.4 − 1) ÷ (1.4 × 1) = (2.639 − 1) ÷ 1.4 = 1.1707. So η = 1 − 0.3147 × 1.1707 = **63.2%**. Diesels are efficient because their large r (no knocking, since only air is compressed) beats the extra penalty from the constant-pressure heating.
/EX

### Real engines and real devices
- **Real petrol engines: 25–35%; real diesels: 35–45%.** The ideal Otto formula gives 56–60%. The gap comes from heat lost to the cylinder walls, friction, incomplete combustion, pumping losses and finite speed (the processes are not quasi-static).
- **Power stations:** steam at 550 °C (823 K), condenser at 27 °C (300 K): Carnot limit 1 − 300 ÷ 823 = **63.5%**. Real coal plants reach about 38%, combined-cycle gas plants about 60%.
- **Refrigerator:** COP = Q_C ÷ W; the Carnot limit is T_C ÷ (T_H − T_C). **Heat pump:** COP = Q_H ÷ W, Carnot limit T_H ÷ (T_H − T_C). Between 0 °C outdoors and 20 °C indoors (273 K and 293 K) the ideal heat pump has COP = 293 ÷ 20 = **14.65**; real ones achieve 3–4. With COP 3.5, delivering 10 kWh of heat costs 10 ÷ 3.5 = **2.86 kWh** of electricity, a third of electric heating.

### Entropy produced by a real engine
EX: How much is lost and where
A real engine takes Q_H = 1000 J from a reservoir at 800 K and rejects heat to 300 K with an efficiency of 35%.
1. W = 0.35 × 1000 = **350 J**, Q_C = **650 J**.
2. Entropy change: reservoir H loses 1000 ÷ 800 = 1.25 J/K, reservoir C gains 650 ÷ 300 = 2.167 J/K. Total **ΔS = 0.917 J/K > 0**.
3. A Carnot engine would give W = 1000 × (1 − 300 ÷ 800) = **625 J**. The work lost to irreversibility is 625 − 350 = **275 J**, and indeed **T_C × ΔS = 300 × 0.917 = 275 J** ✓. This is the key idea: **lost work = T_cold × entropy produced**.
/EX

TRAP: Efficiency formulas for Otto and Diesel use **γ = 1.4** and the compression ratio r, not the pressure ratio. Temperatures must be **absolute (kelvin)** in Carnot and COP formulas. And the Carnot limit is a limit for **any** engine between the two reservoirs; real engines have **two** limits to blame: the Carnot (second law) and irreversibility (friction, finite speed).
