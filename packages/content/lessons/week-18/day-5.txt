# Equilibria, stability and harvesting. Then: RC charging and capacitors in everyday devices
lead: You do not always need the full solution. For an equation y′ = f (y) you can read the whole long-term story from the sign of f: where the system settles, where it is balanced on a knife edge, and what happens when a parameter crosses a critical value. This is how ecologists find that a fishery can collapse suddenly. In physics, you charge and discharge a capacitor through a resistor: the RC time constant, which sets the speed of every digital circuit, touch screen, camera flash and memory chip in your devices.

BN: সবসময় পুরো সমাধান লাগে না। y′ = f(y) সমীকরণে f এর চিহ্ন দেখেই দীর্ঘমেয়াদি গল্প পড়া যায়: ব্যবস্থা কোথায় থিতু হয়, কোথায় ছুরির ধারে ভারসাম্যে থাকে, আর কোনো প্যারামিটার সংকট মান পার হলে কী হয়। এভাবেই পরিবেশবিদরা দেখেন মাছের ভাণ্ডার হঠাৎ ধসে যেতে পারে। পদার্থবিজ্ঞানে রোধের মধ্য দিয়ে ধারক চার্জ ও ডিসচার্জ করবে: RC সময় ধ্রুবক, যা তোমার ডিভাইসের প্রতিটি ডিজিটাল সার্কিট, টাচ স্ক্রিন, ক্যামেরা ফ্ল্যাশ ও মেমরি চিপের গতি ঠিক করে।

@section math | 2 · Math: equilibria and stability | 55 min

### Autonomous equations
An **autonomous** equation has no x (or t) on the right: **y′ = f (y)**. The long-term behaviour comes from a **phase line**:

1. **Equilibria:** the constant solutions y* where f (y*) = 0.
2. **Direction:** between equilibria, y increases where f > 0 and decreases where f < 0.
3. **Stability:** an equilibrium is **stable** if f′ (y*) < 0 (nearby solutions move towards it) and **unstable** if f′ (y*) > 0 (they move away).

BN: স্বয়ংক্রিয় সমীকরণ y′ = f(y): সাম্যাবস্থা f(y*) = 0; f > 0 হলে y বাড়ে, f < 0 হলে কমে। f′(y*) < 0 হলে স্থিতিশীল (কাছের সমাধান ঐদিকে আসে), f′(y*) > 0 হলে অস্থিতিশীল (দূরে সরে যায়)।

EX: Phase lines
1. **y′ = y (2 − y).** Equilibria 0 and 2. f′ (y) = 2 − 2y: f′ (0) = 2 > 0, **unstable**; f′ (2) = −2 < 0, **stable**. Start at 0.1: it grows to 2. Start at 3: it falls to 2. Start at −0.1: it falls away to −∞.
2. **y′ = y² − 4.** Equilibria ±2. f′ = 2y: at y = 2, 4 > 0, **unstable**; at y = −2, −4 < 0, **stable**. A solution starting above 2 blows up in finite time; between −2 and 2 it slides down to −2.
3. **y′ = −y³.** The only equilibrium is 0 and f′ (0) = 0 (the test is silent), but the sign of f says: stable. Solving: dy ÷ y³ = −dt gives y² = 1 ÷ (2t + 1) with y (0) = 1. At t = 4: **y = 1/3**. The decay is **algebraic** (≈ t^(−1/2)), much slower than exponential.
4. **No uniqueness sometimes:** y′ = √y with y (0) = 0 has the solutions y = 0 and y = t²/4 (check: y′ = t/2 = √y ✓). The function √y is not smooth at 0, and a nice (Lipschitz) f is needed for a unique solution.
/EX

### Harvesting a population
Fish are caught at a constant rate H: dP/dt = r P (1 − P ÷ K) − H. Set the right side to zero: r P − r P² ÷ K = H, i.e. P² − K P + (K H ÷ r) = 0:

EQ: P* = [K ± √(K² − 4 K H ÷ r)] ÷ 2     (real only if H ≤ r K ÷ 4)

EX: A fishery
r = 0.5 per year, K = 1000 tonnes of fish, H = 100 tonnes/year.
1. **Equilibria:** P* = [1000 ± √(10⁶ − 8 × 10⁵)] ÷ 2 = (1000 ± 447.2) ÷ 2 = **723.6** and **276.4**.
2. **Stability:** the upper one is **stable** (f falls through zero going up) and the lower one is **unstable**. If the stock starts above 276.4 it recovers towards 723.6; if it starts below 276.4 it **collapses to zero**, because the catch exceeds the growth.
3. **The maximum sustainable harvest** is H = r K ÷ 4 = **125 tonnes/year**, at P = K/2 = 500. At H = 125 the two equilibria merge at 500 (a "half-stable" point: a fish stock at 500 is balanced on a knife edge).
4. **At H = 150** there is **no equilibrium**: dP/dt < 0 for every P, and the stock goes extinct, in finite time. The fishery **collapses suddenly** when H crosses 125: a change of 25% in the catch turns a healthy stock into an extinct one. (This kind of change at a critical parameter value is called a **bifurcation**; the collapse of the Atlantic cod around 1992 is a real example of overfishing.)
/EX

TRAP: The stability test f′ (y*) < 0 fails when f′ (y*) = 0: then look at the **sign of f** on both sides. An equilibrium is stable only if **nearby** solutions approach it, not if the solution happens to stay there. And in the harvest model, **stable equilibria can disappear** when a parameter changes: a population that looks stable for years can crash after a small increase in the catch.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: RC circuits in practice | 35 min

### Charging and discharging
A capacitor C is charged through a resistor R from a battery V₀ (the equation from Day 3: R dq/dt + q ÷ C = V₀):

EQ: V_C (t) = V₀ (1 − e^(−t/RC))     I (t) = (V₀ ÷ R) e^(−t/RC)     τ = R C

Discharging from V₀ through R: V_C = V₀ e^(−t/RC). After **τ** the charging capacitor is at **63.2%**, after **2.3τ** 90%, after **3τ** 95%, after **5τ** 99.3%.

BN: ব্যাটারি V₀ থেকে R এর মধ্য দিয়ে C চার্জ: V_C = V₀(1 − e^(−t/RC)), I = (V₀/R)e^(−t/RC), τ = RC। τ পরে 63.2%, 2.3τ এ 90%, 3τ এ 95%, 5τ এ 99.3%। ডিসচার্জে V_C = V₀e^(−t/RC)।

EX: RC numbers
1. **R = 10 kΩ and C = 100 μF:** τ = 10⁴ × 10⁻⁴ = **1 s**. With V₀ = 12 V, at t = 2 s: V_C = 12 (1 − e⁻²) = **10.4 V**.
2. **Time to reach 90%:** t = τ ln 10 = **2.30 s**. To reach half: t = τ ln 2 = **0.693 s**.
3. **Current:** I (0) = 12 ÷ 10⁴ = **1.2 mA**; at t = 1 s: 1.2 × e⁻¹ = **0.44 mA**. The current starts at its maximum and decays to zero.
4. **Discharge** from 12 V: at t = 3 s V = 12 e⁻³ = **0.60 V**.
5. **Energy:** whatever R is, the heat in the resistor while charging is ½ C V₀² (Day 3).
6. **A low-pass filter:** the same RC circuit passes slow signals and blocks fast ones. Its cutoff frequency is f_c = 1 ÷ (2π R C). For R = 1.6 kΩ and C = 100 nF: **995 Hz** (the topic of Week 20).
7. **A switch debouncer:** a mechanical switch bounces for about 1 ms. With R = 10 kΩ and C = 0.1 μF (τ = 1 ms) the voltage rises smoothly instead of flickering.
/EX

### Capacitors in the devices around you
EX: Capacitance at work
1. **DRAM memory:** each bit is a tiny capacitor of about 25 fF (25 × 10⁻¹⁵ F) charged to 1 V. The charge is Q = 25 fC, only **156,000 electrons**, and the energy 12.5 fJ. Leakage of about 40 fA drains 10% of the charge in 0.1 × 25 fC ÷ 40 fA = **62 ms**, so every cell must be **refreshed** about every 64 ms: this is why the memory is called "dynamic".
2. **Touch screens:** the screen has a grid of electrodes with a small mutual capacitance (about 1 pF). A finger takes some of the field and reduces it by around 10%. The controller measures the RC time constant of each node: with R = 1 MΩ, τ = 1 μs, and a 10% change is 0.1 μs, easily timed by a 100 MHz clock (10 ns). The place where τ changes is where you touch.
3. **A capacitive keyboard or sensor:** a pressed key moves a plate from 4 mm to 2 mm: for A = 0.5 cm² the capacitance goes from **0.11 pF** to **0.22 pF**, doubling. A condenser microphone works the same way: sound pressure moves one plate by a tiny distance, which changes C and so the voltage.
4. **A power supply:** a large capacitor smooths the rectified voltage (the "ripple"): it holds the load while the input dips. The ripple falls with C: ΔV ≈ I ÷ (f C) (Week 20).
5. **The flash and the defibrillator** (Day 3): a slow charge through a resistor or a circuit with τ of seconds, then a very fast discharge through a small resistance.
/EX

TRAP: The time constant is **R C**, not 1 ÷ (R C) and not R + C. The capacitor does not charge **linearly**: it covers 63% of the remaining gap in each τ, and it takes about 5τ to be "fully" charged. Check **units**: kΩ × μF = ms (10³ × 10⁻⁶ = 10⁻³ s). And the current in the circuit is largest at the start, when the capacitor is empty and acts like a wire; it acts like an open circuit when full.
