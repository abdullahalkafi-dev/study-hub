# Separable equations, logistic growth and direction fields. Then: capacitors in series and parallel
lead: Many equations of change can be solved by moving everything with y to one side and everything with x (or t) to the other, then integrating: that is separation of variables. It handles falling with air resistance, populations that level off, and chemical reactions. When no formula exists you can still draw the behaviour with a direction field. In physics, you combine capacitors: side by side they add, one after another they share the voltage, and a surprising amount of energy disappears when a charged capacitor is connected to an empty one.

BN: পরিবর্তনের অনেক সমীকরণ সমাধান করা যায় y সংক্রান্ত সব একপাশে আর x (বা t) সংক্রান্ত সব অন্যপাশে এনে যোগজ করে: একে চলক পৃথকীকরণ বলে। এতে বাতাসের বাধায় পতন, সীমায় থেমে যাওয়া জনসংখ্যা এবং রাসায়নিক বিক্রিয়ার সমাধান হয়। সূত্র না থাকলেও দিকক্ষেত্র (direction field) দিয়ে আচরণ আঁকা যায়। পদার্থবিজ্ঞানে ধারক জুড়বে: পাশাপাশি যোগ হয়, একটার পর একটা ভোল্টেজ ভাগ করে, আর চার্জিত ধারক খালি ধারকের সাথে জুড়লে আশ্চর্যজনক পরিমাণ শক্তি হারিয়ে যায়।

@section math | 2 · Math: separable equations | 55 min

### The method
If the equation can be written dy/dx = g (x) h (y), separate and integrate:

EQ: ∫ dy ÷ h (y) = ∫ g (x) dx + C

Solve for y if you can; use the initial condition to fix C. Remember the **constant solutions** (where h (y) = 0), which separation can lose.

EX: Four separable equations
1. **dy/dx = x y, y (0) = 2.** ∫ dy ÷ y = ∫ x dx gives ln y = x²/2 + C, so y = A e^(x²/2); A = 2. **y = 2 e^(x²/2)**, and y (1) = 2 e^0.5 = **3.297**.
2. **dy/dx = x ÷ y, y (0) = 2.** ∫ y dy = ∫ x dx gives y²/2 = x²/2 + C, so y² = x² + 4 (C from y (0) = 2). **y = √(x² + 4)**, y (3) = √13 = **3.606**.
3. **dy/dx = y², y (0) = 1.** ∫ dy ÷ y² = ∫ dx gives −1 ÷ y = x + C, and C = −1: **y = 1 ÷ (1 − x)**. At x = 0.5, y = 2. As x → 1 the solution **blows up to infinity in finite time**: a warning that fast growth rates (y² rather than y) behave very differently from exponential.
4. **Terminal velocity.** A skydiver (m = 70 kg) with linear drag: m dv/dt = m g − b v, with b = 10 kg/s. Write dv ÷ (g − (b/m) v) = dt and integrate: **v (t) = (m g ÷ b) (1 − e^(−b t/m))**. The terminal speed is m g ÷ b = 70 × 9.8 ÷ 10 = **68.6 m/s**, with time constant m ÷ b = **7 s**. After 7 s: v = 68.6 × (1 − e⁻¹) = **43.4 m/s**.
/EX

### Logistic growth
Unlimited exponential growth cannot last: food, space or customers run out. The **logistic equation** has growth rate r at small sizes and zero growth at the **carrying capacity** K:

EQ: dP/dt = r P (1 − P ÷ K)     ⟹     P (t) = K ÷ [1 + ((K − P₀) ÷ P₀) e^(−r t)]

It is separable (use partial fractions). The curve is an S-shape (a sigmoid): slow, then fast, then levelling at K. The fastest growth is at **P = K/2**, with rate **r K ÷ 4**.

EX: A population
K = 1000, P₀ = 100 and r = 0.5 per year: P (t) = 1000 ÷ (1 + 9 e^(−0.5 t)).
1. **At t = 5:** e^(−2.5) = 0.0821, so P = 1000 ÷ (1 + 0.739) = **575**.
2. **When does it reach 500?** 1 + 9 e^(−0.5t) = 2, e^(−0.5t) = 1/9, **t = 2 ln 9 = 4.39 years**.
3. **The fastest growth** is at P = 500 (t = 4.39 years), at the rate 0.5 × 1000 ÷ 4 = **125 per year**.
4. **Another case:** dP/dt = 0.02 P (1 − P ÷ 500) with P₀ = 50: P (100) = 500 ÷ (1 + 9 e^(−2)) = **225**.
5. **An epidemic or a rumour** in a town of 10,000 with 10 infected and r = 0.4 per day: P = 10000 ÷ (1 + 999 e^(−0.4 t)). Half the town is reached at t = ln 999 ÷ 0.4 = **17.3 days**, when the spread is the fastest, at r K ÷ 4 = **1000 new cases per day**. (This model is also used for how fast new technology is adopted.)
/EX

### Direction fields
Even when you cannot solve dy/dx = f (x, y), you can draw small slope segments at many points: a **direction field**. Solution curves follow the segments. Where f = 0 the segments are horizontal.

EX: Reading a direction field
1. **dy/dx = x − y.** At (0, 0) the slope is 0; at (1, 0) it is **1**; at (0, 1) it is **−1**; at (2, 2) it is **0**. The slopes are zero along the line y = x (an **isocline** with slope 0). A solution starting at (0, 1) goes down at first, then bends up towards the line y = x − 1 (the solution which is a straight line: y = x − 1 gives y′ = 1 = x − (x − 1) ✓).
2. **dy/dt = r y (1 − y ÷ K):** the segments are horizontal at y = 0 and y = K. Between them they point up: all solutions starting there climb to K. Above K they point down. So y = K is **stable** and y = 0 is **unstable**.
3. **Equilibria:** a constant solution y = c where f (c) = 0. It is stable if nearby solutions approach it.
/EX

TRAP: When you divide by h (y) you may **lose a solution** where h (y) = 0 (for the logistic equation, P = 0 and P = K). Do not forget the **constant** of integration before you apply the initial condition (put it in at the integration step). And when you take the exponential of ln y = x²/2 + C, the constant becomes a **factor**: y = A e^(x²/2), not y = e^(x²/2) + A.

@section physics | 3 · Physics: capacitors in series and parallel | 35 min

### Parallel
Capacitors connected side by side (same V across each) act like one large plate: charges add and so do capacitances.

EQ: C_parallel = C₁ + C₂ + …     (same V; Q_total = Σ Qᵢ)

### Series
Capacitors connected end to end (same Q on each, since the middle plates are isolated) share the voltage:

EQ: 1 ÷ C_series = 1 ÷ C₁ + 1 ÷ C₂ + …     (same Q; V_total = Σ Vᵢ)

The series total is always **smaller than the smallest** capacitor (a thicker effective gap).

BN: সমান্তরাল: প্রতিটিতে একই V; C = C₁ + C₂ + …। শ্রেণি: প্রতিটিতে একই Q; 1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + …; ফল সবচেয়ে ছোট ধারকের চেয়েও ছোট। (রোধের সূত্রের ঠিক উল্টো।)

EX: Combinations
1. **2 μF and 3 μF in parallel:** 5 μF. **In series:** 1 ÷ C = 1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6, **C = 1.2 μF**.
2. **12 V across the series pair:** Q = C V = 1.2 × 12 = **14.4 μC** on each capacitor. V₁ = Q ÷ C₁ = 14.4 ÷ 2 = **7.2 V**, V₂ = 14.4 ÷ 3 = **4.8 V**: the **smaller** capacitor gets the larger voltage. The sum is 12 V ✓.
3. **12 V across the parallel pair:** Q₁ = 2 × 12 = **24 μC**, Q₂ = 3 × 12 = **36 μC**, total 60 μC = 5 μF × 12 V ✓.
4. **A network:** C₁ = 4 μF in series with (2 μF ∥ 6 μF). The parallel part is 8 μF. Series: 1 ÷ C = 1/4 + 1/8 = 3/8, **C = 2.667 μF**. With 24 V: Q = 2.667 × 24 = **64 μC**. V across C₁: 64 ÷ 4 = **16 V**; across the pair: 64 ÷ 8 = **8 V**. The charge on the 2 μF is 2 × 8 = **16 μC** and on the 6 μF is **48 μC** (16 + 48 = 64 ✓).
5. **Three identical 10 μF:** in series **3.33 μF**; in parallel **30 μF**.
6. **Voltage rating:** two 100 μF, 50 V capacitors in series make 50 μF rated at 100 V (ideally); in parallel they make 200 μF rated at 50 V.
7. **Charge sharing.** A 6 μF capacitor charged to 12 V (Q = 72 μC) is connected to an uncharged 3 μF. The charge is conserved and the voltage becomes V = 72 ÷ 9 = **8 V**. Energy before: ½ × 6 × 10⁻⁶ × 144 = **432 μJ**. After: ½ × 9 × 10⁻⁶ × 64 = **288 μJ**. The missing **144 μJ** (one third) went into the spark, heat and radiation in the wires: **energy is not conserved in the capacitors alone**.
/EX

TRAP: Capacitors combine **opposite** to resistors: parallel capacitances **add**, series ones add as **reciprocals**. In a series connection the charge is the **same** on every capacitor, while the voltages differ; in parallel the voltage is the same while the charges differ. And never assume energy is conserved when you connect capacitors at different voltages: the lost energy shows up as heat and radiation.
