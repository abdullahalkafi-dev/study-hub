# Linear first-order equations and the integrating factor. Then: energy in capacitors and the camera flash
lead: Many real systems follow a linear first-order equation: a tank being filled with salty water, a drug being infused into the blood, a loan being repaid, a capacitor being charged. They all have the form "the rate of change of y is a push minus a leak proportional to y". The integrating factor turns the left side into a single derivative and solves them all in one move. In physics, you learn why a capacitor is the heart of a camera flash and a defibrillator: it stores energy slowly and releases it in a thousandth of a second.

BN: অনেক বাস্তব ব্যবস্থা রৈখিক প্রথম ক্রমের সমীকরণ মেনে চলে: লবণ-পানিতে ভরা ট্যাংক, রক্তে ওষুধের ইনফিউশন, ঋণ পরিশোধ, ধারকের চার্জ হওয়া। সবার আকার একই: "y এর পরিবর্তনের হার = একটা ধাক্কা − y এর সমানুপাতিক ক্ষরণ"। সমাকলন গুণক বাম পাশকে একটা অবকলে নামিয়ে সবগুলো এক চালেই সমাধান করে। পদার্থবিজ্ঞানে বুঝবে কেন ধারকই ক্যামেরা ফ্ল্যাশ ও ডিফিব্রিলেটরের হৃদয়: সে ধীরে শক্তি জমায় আর হাজার ভাগের এক সেকেন্ডে ছাড়ে।

@section math | 2 · Math: the integrating factor | 55 min

### The method
A **linear first-order** equation has the form

EQ: y′ + p (x) y = q (x)

Multiply by the **integrating factor** μ (x) = e^(∫ p dx). By the product rule, (μ y)′ = μ y′ + μ′ y = μ (y′ + p y) because μ′ = p μ. So the equation becomes **(μ y)′ = μ q**, which you integrate directly:

EQ: y = (1 ÷ μ) [ ∫ μ q dx + C ]

BN: y′ + p(x)y = q(x) আকারের সমীকরণে μ = e^(∫p dx) দিয়ে গুণ করলে বাম পাশ হয় (μy)′ (গুণফলের নিয়মে), তাই (μy)′ = μq, যোগজ করে y = (1/μ)[∫μq dx + C]।

EX: Four equations
1. **y′ + 2y = 6, y (0) = 0.** μ = e^(2x). (e^(2x) y)′ = 6 e^(2x), so e^(2x) y = 3 e^(2x) + C and y = 3 + C e^(−2x). y (0) = 0 gives C = −3: **y = 3 (1 − e^(−2x))**. y (1) = 3 × 0.8647 = **2.594**. (It rises to the steady value 3.)
2. **y′ + y ÷ x = x², x > 0, y (1) = 1.** μ = e^(ln x) = x. (x y)′ = x³, so x y = x⁴/4 + C, y = x³/4 + C ÷ x. y (1) = 1 gives C = 3/4: **y = x³/4 + 3 ÷ (4x)**, and y (2) = 2 + 0.375 = **2.375**.
3. **y′ − y = e^(2x), y (0) = 0.** μ = e^(−x). (e^(−x) y)′ = e^x, so e^(−x) y = e^x + C and y = e^(2x) + C e^x. C = −1: **y = e^(2x) − e^x**, y (1) = e² − e = **4.671**.
4. **y′ + y = sin x, y (0) = 0.** μ = e^x: (e^x y)′ = e^x sin x, and ∫ e^x sin x dx = (e^x ÷ 2)(sin x − cos x). So y = (sin x − cos x) ÷ 2 + C e^(−x), C = 1/2: **y = (sin x − cos x + e^(−x)) ÷ 2**. After a short **transient** (the e^(−x) term) it settles into a steady oscillation ½ (sin x − cos x): the pattern of every driven circuit (Week 20).
/EX

### Mixing, infusion and loans
EX: The same equation everywhere
1. **Mixing tank.** 100 L of solution holds 10 kg of salt. Salt water at 0.5 kg/L flows in at 4 L/min, and the mixture flows out at 4 L/min. dS/dt = 4 × 0.5 − 4 × S/100 = 2 − 0.04 S. The solution: **S (t) = 50 − 40 e^(−0.04 t)**. At 30 min: 50 − 40 e^(−1.2) = **37.95 kg**. It tends to 50 kg (0.5 kg/L × 100 L). The salt reaches 40 kg when e^(−0.04 t) = 1/4, at **t = 34.7 min**.
2. **Drug infusion.** A drug is infused at R = 10 mg/h into a body of V = 40 L that eliminates a fraction k = 0.1 per hour. dC/dt = R ÷ V − k C = 0.25 − 0.1 C. The steady concentration is R ÷ (k V) = **2.5 mg/L**, reached as C (t) = 2.5 (1 − e^(−0.1 t)). At 10 h: **1.58 mg/L** (63% of the steady value).
3. **A loan.** B dollars grow at 6% per year (continuous) and you pay 2400 per year: dB/dt = 0.06 B − 2400. With B (0) = 10,000: B (t) = 40,000 − 30,000 e^(0.06 t). It is paid off when e^(0.06 t) = 4/3: **t = ln (4/3) ÷ 0.06 = 4.8 years**. (If the payment were only 600 per year, the debt grows forever: 600 ÷ 0.06 = 10,000 is the threshold.)
4. **A capacitor charging** (preview of Week 19): R dq/dt + q ÷ C = V₀. In standard form dq/dt + q ÷ (R C) = V₀ ÷ R, so q (t) = C V₀ (1 − e^(−t/RC)). For R = 1 kΩ, C = 100 μF (τ = R C = 0.1 s) and V₀ = 12 V: at t = 0.2 s the capacitor is at 12 × (1 − e^(−2)) = **10.4 V**.
/EX

@section physics | 3 · Physics: energy in capacitors and the camera flash | 35 min

### Energy stored
Moving charge dq onto a capacitor at voltage V = q ÷ C costs dU = V dq = (q ÷ C) dq. Adding up from 0 to Q:

EQ: U = ½ C V² = ½ Q V = Q² ÷ (2C)

It is the energy of the **field** between the plates (Week 17: u = ½ ε₀ E²).

BN: ধারকে জমা শক্তি U = ½CV² = ½QV = Q²/2C। এটা প্লেটের মাঝের ক্ষেত্রে জমা থাকে (u = ½ε₀E²)।

EX: Energy numbers
1. **100 μF at 12 V:** U = 0.5 × 100 × 10⁻⁶ × 144 = **7.2 mJ**. **1 μF at 100 V:** **5 mJ**.
2. **A camera flash:** 100 μF charged to 300 V stores U = 0.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 9 × 10⁴ = **4.5 J** and Q = C V = **30 mC**.
3. **Why a capacitor?** Discharged through the lamp in 1 ms, the power is 4.5 J ÷ 10⁻³ s = **4500 W**. Two AA cells (3 V, 1 A at most, about 3 W) could never deliver that directly. A circuit charges the capacitor slowly (4.5 J ÷ 3 W = **1.5 s** in the ideal case, a few seconds in real life) and the capacitor releases it quickly. Energy is stored slowly and spent fast: **power is stretched in time**.
4. **A defibrillator** delivers about 200 J. With C = 100 μF it needs U = ½ C V²: V = √(2 × 200 ÷ 10⁻⁴) = **2000 V**, Q = C V = **0.2 C**, in a pulse of about 5 ms (average power 40 kW).
5. **A supercapacitor** of 3000 F at 2.7 V stores 0.5 × 3000 × 2.7² = **10.9 kJ**, about the energy of one AA battery (2000 mAh × 1.5 V × 3600 = 10.8 kJ). But it can be charged in seconds, and delivers hundreds of amps. A 100 μF, 300 V capacitor holds **2400 times** less than that AA battery.
6. **A 10 F supercapacitor at 5 V:** 0.5 × 10 × 25 = **125 J**, enough to lift a 1 kg mass 12.8 m.
/EX

### Force between the plates
The plates attract each other. For fixed charge Q the energy is U = Q² d ÷ (2 ε₀ A), which grows with the gap d, so the force is F = −dU/dd (with sign: it pulls the plates together):

EQ: F = Q² ÷ (2 ε₀ A) = ½ Q E = ½ ε₀ A E²

The ½ appears because each plate feels only the field of the **other** plate (E ÷ 2), not its own.

EX: Force and work
1. **A = 0.02 m², Q = 1 μC:** F = 10⁻¹² ÷ (2 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.02) = **2.83 N**.
2. **Doubling the gap** from 1 mm to 2 mm at fixed Q costs W = F Δd = 2.83 × 10⁻³ = **2.83 mJ**. Check: U = Q² d ÷ (2 ε₀ A): 2.83 mJ at 1 mm and 5.65 mJ at 2 mm ✓. The voltage doubles as well: V = Q ÷ C and C halves.
3. **Charging from a battery (the half that is lost).** The battery moves a charge Q = C V through a voltage V, so it gives Q V = C V². The capacitor stores only ½ C V²: **half the energy is lost as heat in the resistance** (whatever its size): for C = 100 μF and V = 12 V the battery gives 14.4 mJ, the capacitor holds 7.2 mJ, and 7.2 mJ becomes heat.
4. **Discharging through a resistor:** the whole ½ C V² becomes heat (in the lamp, in a flash bulb as light and heat).
/EX

TRAP: Energy and charge are **different**: doubling V doubles Q but makes the energy **four times** larger. In a battery-charged capacitor, the energy from the battery is C V², and **half** is lost, so efficiency tricks like "charge it more slowly" do not help unless you change the way the voltage is applied. When connecting charged capacitors, the lost energy is not a violation of anything: it is radiated or heated in the wires.
